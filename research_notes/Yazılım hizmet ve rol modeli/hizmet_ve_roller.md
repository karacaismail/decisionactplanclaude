## A1. Evidence on adoption failure of strategy and OKR software, and the role of sponsorship, cadence and facilitation

### Takeaway
The evidence is consistent in direction but weak in quality. Strategy fails mostly in execution and governance, not in tooling. Active executive sponsorship and a fixed review cadence are the most cited success factors. No rigorous, independent study isolates "software alone" versus "software plus facilitation" for strategy tools.

### Cited Findings
- EIU survey for the Brightline Initiative, 2017: 500 senior executives at companies with revenue of at least 1 billion USD, fielded June and July 2017. On average, organizations fail to meet 20% of strategic objectives because of poor implementation. 59% say they often struggle to bridge strategy development and day-to-day implementation. 62% say implementation is seen as an operational task rather than a strategic one. Only about 1 in 10 ("Leaders") said delivery failures blocked no strategic goal over three years — [Brightline press release](https://www.brightline.org/press/releases/companies-with-great-strategies-often-fall-short-on-execution-says-economist-intelligence-unit-survey/); [Brightline EIU report page](https://www.brightline.org/resources/eiu-report/) (search summary).
- PMI and EIU, 2016: only 61% of projects key to putting strategy in place yielded the intended strategic benefits, survey of more than 500 executives — [PMI thought leadership page](https://www.pmi.org/learning/thought-leadership/series/benefits-realization/strengthening-benefits-awareness-csuite-eiu) (search summary). An older "56% of strategic initiatives implemented" figure attributed to EIU 2013 could not be verified ([Medium post, secondary](https://medium.com/@sdoniger/most-of-your-organizations-strategic-initiatives-fail-b167922bf594)).
- The widely quoted "60% to 90% of strategies fail" range is an aggregate across studies, not a single primary result — [Corporate Finance Institute](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/strategy/why-does-strategy-fail/) (search summary).
- OKR Institute names the absence of active executive sponsorship as the most common reason OKRs fail. Poor OKR writing and lack of a regular check-in cadence follow closely. This is a training vendor, not independent research — [OKR Institute, Why OKRs fail](https://okrinstitute.org/why-okrs-fail/) (search summary).
- OKR Institute says a rollout from pilot to full adoption typically takes 9 to 12 months, and compressing it into one quarter often leads to failure — [OKR Institute rollout guide](https://okrinstitute.org/okr-implementation-how-to-roll-out-okrs-across-departments/) (search summary).
- A buyer's guide argues that software does not drive adoption. It says buyers should first decide whether an executive sponsor will actively champion the process, and that choosing tooling before governance is a common mistake — [Serchen OKR software buyer's guide](https://www.serchen.com/blog/okr-software-buyers-guide) (search summary).
- Prosci change-management data, cited secondhand: 73% success for initiatives with active and visible executive sponsorship versus 29% without. This is a general change-management figure, not strategy-software specific — [Mooncamp glossary citing Prosci](https://mooncamp.com/glossary/change-adoption) (search summary, secondary; Mooncamp is an OKR vendor).
- A claim that weekly OKR review makes goal achievement 43% more likely appeared without a primary source. Do not use it as fact — [OKR Hub](https://www.theokrhub.com/insights/why-okrs-fail) (search summary, unsourced).
- The repository already records a related vendor claim: software spreads poorly when no separate role runs the strategy work — R2 section 3.3, citing [Mooncamp](https://mooncamp.com/blog/cascade-alternatives).
- The repository's own pilot hypothesis lists the typical blocker "team members do not enter data, the form is found long", and lists "the execution rhythm dies over time" as the typical blocker at rollout — R2 section 6, steps 5 and 7.

### Inferences
- Inference: The product's design already encodes the main success factors. One named karar sahibi per plan is the sponsor role. The fixed 3.5 review ("Is the choice still right?") is the cadence anchor. The stratej is the facilitator role. The risk is not missing features. The risk is that nobody fills the stratej role, or that the review cadence lapses.
- Inference: Because the repository's ICP often has no internal stratej (R2 section 3.3), the software cannot rely on a self-serve rollout for this segment. Someone must run the first cycle.

### Gaps
- No independent, primary study was found that compares strategy-tool outcomes with and without facilitation or coaching.
- No Gartner or Forrester primary page on strategy-execution software adoption was retrieved. Gartner pages were not fetched.
- No Turkish data on adoption of strategy or OKR software was found.

## A3. Benchmarks for services share of revenue, margin effects, productized services and partner-led models

### Takeaway
Secondary benchmarks put the median B2B SaaS services share at about 15% of revenue. Services gross margin runs near 30%, against about 80% for subscription. Services above about 15% of revenue visibly lower blended margin. Primary KeyBanc and SaaS Capital reports were not retrieved, so the numbers need checking.

### Cited Findings
- Benchmarkit figures, as reported secondhand: professional services gross margin median about 30% versus about 81% for subscription. Services are about 15% of total revenue at the median company — [Orb, SaaS gross margin statistics](https://www.withorb.com/blog/saas-growth-margin-statistics) (search summary, secondary).
- Illustration: 80% subscription margin and 25% services margin, with services at 30% of revenue, gives about 65% to 68% blended gross margin — [b2bcentr, 2026 SaaS gross margin report](https://www.b2bcentr.com/saas-gross-margin-2026/) (search summary, secondary).
- KeyBanc survey write-ups disagree on the median total gross margin (about 75% for 2025 in one, 71% total and 79% subscription for 2024 in another). One write-up says companies with more than 15% services revenue show meaningfully lower gross margins and that the survey recommends minimizing services revenue — [Seeto, KeyBanc SaaS survey 2025 takeaways](https://seeto.ai/blog/keybanc-saas-survey-2025-takeaways) (search summary, secondary, not verified against the primary report).
- SaaS Capital 2026 survey, as reported secondhand: hosting about 5% of ARR, DevOps about 4%, professional-services COGS about 5%, other COGS about 3%, total COGS about 17% of ARR — [Layerz, SaaS spending benchmarks](https://layerz.cc/resources/saas-spending-benchmarks) (search summary, secondary).
- Cascade's partner model shows a partner-led alternative: the consultant sells and delivers the service and the vendor keeps the subscription — [Cascade for partners](https://www.cascade.app/partners) (search summary).

### Inferences
- Inference: For this product, services should exist but should mostly be delivered by partners. The vendor should sell a small fixed-scope "productized" package: for example the 8-week single-decision pilot with three defined outputs (transition decision, handover report, approved strategy document; R2 section 6). This keeps vendor services near or below the 15% median.
- Inference: A productized package has a fixed scope, fixed price and fixed outputs. This is what keeps its margin closer to software margin than open-ended consulting. This is a standard reasoning point, not a sourced benchmark.
- Inference: Partner facilitation revenue does not hit the vendor's margin. The vendor's cost becomes partner enablement: certification, templates and a partner view.

### Gaps
- Primary KeyBanc (KBCM and Sapphire Ventures) and SaaS Capital reports were not retrieved. All figures above are secondary.
- No benchmark specific to Turkish SaaS companies or to strategy software was found.
- No data was found on margins of "productized services" as a separate category.

## A4. What Turkish mid-size family firms likely need, and how consultants can be partners rather than competitors

### Takeaway
Turkish sources describe family-firm institutionalization as consultant-assisted work. A family constitution and a family council are typical tools, and neutral advisors help in disputes. The firm's main problem is often unclear decision ownership and family communication, not software. This supports "software plus facilitation by partner consultants", with the record staying in the firm.

### Cited Findings
- Product fact: the ICP is a Turkish family firm with 50 to 499 employees that faced one bounded, costly strategic decision in the last 12 months, with an internal or external stratej. All numbers are hypotheses — R2 section 4.1.
- Product fact: in mid-size firms an internal stratej often does not exist. The consultant fills this gap and is both channel and part of the ICP — R2 sections 1 and 3.3.
- Product fact: a study of 601 SME managers found intergenerational tension slows digital transformation most — R2 section 3.1, citing [Forbes Türkiye, 4 February 2026](https://www.forbes.com.tr/dergi/kurucular-temkinli-ikinci-kusak-hizli-dijital-donusum-gerilim-yaratiyor).
- Product fact: KOSGEB digital transformation credit does not cover consultancy — R2 section 3.3, citing [erp-software.org](https://erp-software.org/en/erp-worldwide/turkey/).
- Product fact: Turkish SME buyers often talk with three parties, the vendor, the implementing firm and the tax advisor — R2 section 3.3, citing [erp-software.org](https://erp-software.org/en/erp-worldwide/turkey/).
- Product fact: the buying group includes the second-generation general manager, the founder-generation boss, the corporate development manager, the finance manager, IT, legal or KVKK officer, and the family council or board. The family council maps to the Okur role — R2 section 4.6.
- Product fact: a consultant persona fear is "the client starts bypassing the consultant with AI". The consultant's pain is that revenue ends after delivery — R2 section 5.7.
- Turkish columns and guides describe the family constitution as a rule set for family members' relation to the company. They recommend preparing it with external advisory support and approval by family members, then forming a family council to apply it. A neutral consultant, mediator or lawyer may support updates and disputes — [Karar, "Aile anayasası nedir?"](https://www.karar.com/yazarlar/rasit-yildirim/aile-anayasasi-nedir-1597556); [Capital, "Her aileye bir anayasa"](https://www.capital.com.tr/yonetim/liderlik/her-aileye-bir-anayasa); [TKYD, Türkiye'de aile şirketleri için kurumsal yönetim (PDF)](https://www.tkyd.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/TKYD_Turkiyede-Aile-Sirketleri-Icin-Kurumsal-Yonetim_01112021.pdf) (search summary, mostly columnists and advisors).
- One source says most problems in family firms come from communication between family members, not from technical issues. Another says many Turkish family firms fail to pass to the second or third generation because institutionalization is incomplete — [Karar, "Ailede birlik, şirkette süreklilik"](https://www.karar.com/yazarlar/rasit-yildirim/ailede-birlik-sirkette-sureklilik-1593619) (search summary).

### Inferences
- Inference: The family firm needs three services the software cannot provide alone: (1) a facilitator for the 2.4 choice meeting, where the karar sahibi chooses in front of family stakeholders; (2) a neutral second opinion, which the product already models as the Danışman role; (3) a quarterly review ritual that someone schedules and runs.
- Inference: Consultants become partners when the product gives them recurring work and keeps the record in the client firm. The product already says "the record stays in the company, the consultant can use the product too" (R2 section 4.5). A partner offer could be: certification, a referral or resale margin, the Stratej role in client plans, and a multi-client view. The multi-client view does not exist yet (R2 sections 3.3 and 5.7).
- Inference: The "one karar sahibi, recorded by name and time" rule is both a selling point and a fear for the founder generation (R2 section 3.1). A facilitator is the person who manages this tension in the room. Software cannot do that.

### Gaps
- No survey data was found on Turkish family firms' spending on strategy consultants or facilitation.
- No Turkish strategy-software vendor partner program was found to compare with.
- Family-council meeting frequency in Turkish firms was not found in a primary source.

## B1. Which roles must be active, what each must do, at which cadence (RACI mapped to product roles)

### Takeaway
Value needs four active roles at minimum: karar sahibi, stratej, ekip üyesi and, for single founders, a Danışman. Kurum yöneticisi and Okur are low-frequency roles. Execution creates value only if the team writes observations and resource movements weekly, and if the karar sahibi records a review at least monthly and a strategy test at least quarterly. The cadences are recommendations, not sourced benchmarks.

### Cited Findings
- Product fact, roles: Sistem yöneticisi (all sites, operator, release and feature flags); Kurum yöneticisi (tenant: add users, revoke devices, sync settings, cannot open feature flags); Karar sahibi (plan: add plan members, record decisions, open and close resource permits, approve strategy document, review items sent to review); Stratej (plan: proposes paths, writes strategy steps and targets, cannot record decisions); Ekip üyesi (plan: step answers, evidence, targets, observations, resource movements, may give second opinion); Danışman (plan: read and second opinion only); Okur (plan: read only) — R1 section 3.5.
- Product fact: one person may hold several roles. In a single-founder plan karar sahibi and stratej may be the same person. Only one karar sahibi per plan at a time; the old assignment's end is written in the same transaction; the assigner is stored — R1 section 3.5.
- Product fact: second opinion must come from another plan member's own account. A single founder without another member cannot get a second opinion, cannot leave the "İlerle" suggestion and cannot raise a limit. So the founder adds a Danışman — R1 sections 3.5 and 14.
- Product fact: the three clusters are pre-strategy preparation (Karar 1 to 5 and the transition gate), strategy formulation (2.1 to 2.5, ends with strategy approval by karar sahibi), and strategy execution (3.1 to 3.5, a loop without an exit gate) — R3 section 1.
- Product fact: in 2.1, 2.2, 2.3 and 2.5 the stratej writes. In 2.4 the karar sahibi chooses the path, the winning target and the field, and writes what is given up — R3 section 1.
- Product fact: 3.1 and 3.2 are done by the team. 3.3 (money and people) by the karar sahibi. 3.4 is done by the team with Karar 6 and 7. 3.5 is the karar sahibi's "Is the choice still right?" with outcomes Sürdür, Düzelt or Yeniden aç — R3 section 1.
- Product fact: the software does not choose a path, does not decide the transition and does not answer "Is the choice still right?" — R3 section 7.
- Product fact: buying-group mapping: second-generation GM = Karar sahibi or Okur; founder-generation boss = Okur or Karar sahibi; corporate development manager = Stratej or Ekip üyesi; finance manager = Ekip üyesi or Okur; IT = Kurum yöneticisi; family council or board = Okur — R2 section 4.6.

## B2. Minimal active-usage definition and usage signals that predict retention

### Takeaway
No public, sourced data was found on which usage signals predict retention in strategy or OKR tools. A minimal active-plan definition can still be drawn from the product's own records. The proposal below is an inference to be tested in pilots.

### Cited Findings
- Product fact: records exist that can serve as signals: Gözlem (observation, append-only), Kaynak Hareketi (resource movement, append-only), Strateji Gözden Geçirme (strategy review, with "next check date"), Kapı Kararı, Strateji Onayı, Kaynak İzni with status, Cihaz with last sync — R1 sections 5.1 and 5.3.
- Product fact: the repository's pilot hypothesis is 8 weeks for one decision with 2 to 5 team members, producing transition decision, handover report and approved strategy document. Rollout needs the first review record as proof — R2 section 6.
- Product fact: the typical rollout blocker is that the execution rhythm dies over time — R2 section 6, step 7.

### Inferences
- Inference: Proposed "active plan" definition, measured per plan in execution:
  1. At least one observation per active target in the last 30 days.
  2. At least one karar sahibi session and one recorded review (Karar 6 or 7) in the last 31 days.
  3. A 3.5 strategy review recorded no later than its stored "next check date" plus 14 days.
  4. No review item older than 14 days in the karar sahibi's queue.
- Inference: Proposed leading indicators of churn: missed next check date; zero observations for 30 days; karar sahibi inactive for 30 days; stratej or Danışman membership ended without replacement.
- Inference: Proposed "activation" milestone for pre-strategy plans: transition decision recorded within the pilot window (about 8 weeks).
- Inference: Count signals per plan, not per user. Value in this product comes from decisions and observations on a plan, not from logins.

### Gaps
- No vendor or analyst data on retention predictors for strategy or OKR software was found.
- Thresholds (30 days, 14 days) are placeholders to validate in the R2 section 7 interviews and pilots.

## C1. Definitions of RBAC, ABAC and ReBAC, with sources

### Takeaway
RBAC grants by role. ABAC evaluates attributes of subject, object, action and environment against policy. ReBAC grants by relationships between users and objects, and between objects. Zanzibar is the reference ReBAC system. OpenFGA and SpiceDB implement its model. Cedar is a policy language that mixes role hierarchy with attribute conditions.

### Cited Findings
- NIST SP 800-162, "Guide to Attribute Based Access Control (ABAC) Definition and Considerations", January 2014, updated 2 August 2019. ABAC is "a logical access control methodology where authorization to perform a set of operations is determined" by evaluating attributes of subject, object, requested operations and sometimes environment conditions against policy, rules or relationships — [NIST CSRC](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/162/upd2/final).
- OpenFGA definitions: RBAC, "permissions are assigned to users based on roles like editor or admin". ABAC, "permissions are granted based on attributes of the user, resource, or request". ReBAC "makes access rules conditional on relationships between users and objects, and between objects themselves", and is described as a superset of RBAC. PBAC "manages authorization policies centrally, external to application code" — [OpenFGA authorization concepts](https://openfga.dev/docs/authorization-concepts).
- OpenFGA "implements the Zanzibar model on your existing databases" and "extends ReBAC with Conditions and Contextual Tuples" for attribute cases — [OpenFGA authorization concepts](https://openfga.dev/docs/authorization-concepts).
- Google Zanzibar (USENIX ATC 2019): "a global system for storing and evaluating access control lists" with a uniform data model and configuration language for services such as Calendar, Cloud, Drive, Maps, Photos and YouTube. Decisions respect causal ordering of user actions (external consistency). Trillions of ACLs, millions of requests per second, 95th-percentile latency under 10 ms, availability above 99.999% over 3 years — [USENIX, Pang et al. 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/pang).
- Cedar: policies have a mandatory scope of principal, action and resource; effect is permit or forbid; optional when and unless clauses evaluate attributes of principal, resource and request context; entities can have parents and the in operator checks hierarchy membership; the result is allow or deny with the determining policies — [Cedar terminology](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/overview/terminology.html).

### Inferences
- Inference: This product needs all three. Site roles are RBAC. Plan membership, task ownership and authorship are ReBAC. Plan state, gate locks, permit amounts, device revocation and time windows are ABAC.

### Gaps
- Cedar's "forbid overrides permit" and "default deny" semantics were not confirmed from the fetched page. The page points to a separate authorization section that was not fetched.
- SpiceDB (AuthZed), Oso and Permit.io docs were not fetched in this session. Their descriptions are not cited here. The report writer should not attribute specific features to them without checking [AuthZed docs](https://authzed.com/docs), [Oso docs](https://www.osohq.com/docs) and [Permit.io docs](https://docs.permit.io/).
- The Zanzibar paper's "zookie" and "new enemy problem" details were not confirmed from the fetched abstract page.

## C2. Which authorization needs this product has (relations, attributes, separation of duty)

### Takeaway
Most rules depend on a relation to a plan plus a state attribute. Separation-of-duty rules already exist in the repository and must be enforced on the server with identity taken from the access token. The needs are moderate in size per tenant, because each tenant has its own site.

### Cited Findings
- Product fact, relation store: Plan Üyeliği has plan, user, role, start, end, active, assigner. Ended rows keep role history. Karar sahibi assignments do not overlap — R1 section 5.1.
- Product fact, device attribute: Cihaz has revocation fields. A revoked device's keys drop to a narrow scope: send pending changes to review, and receive the "delete local data" command. Nothing else — R1 sections 5.1 and 9.3.
- Product fact, identity: karar sahibi, approver, second-opinion giver, reviewer and assigner come from the access token, never from client fields. The server decides the tenant from the address and the person from the token — R1 sections 9.1 and 9.3.
- Product fact, state attributes: strategy steps cannot be written without a transition decision; execution steps cannot be written while their own decision is locked; "İlerle" cannot be recorded on a locked execution gate; a movement on a closed permit is accepted only into review; a movement above the permit's remainder goes to review with an "overspent" flag — R1 sections 7.2 and 7.3.
- Product fact, threshold attributes: resource allocation needs a valid strategy approval and an amount not above free budget. The budget is computed under a plan lock — R1 section 7.2.
- Product fact, separation of duty: Sınır Değişikliği (limit change) increase needs a second opinion; the reviewer cannot be the person who made the change — R1 section 5.3.
- Product fact, separation of duty: second opinion is given by another plan member with their own account — R1 section 3.5.
- Product fact, decision path: decision doctypes give user roles no direct create or submit permission. Decisions are written only through workflow endpoints. Key checks also run in before_submit as a second layer — R1 section 6.3.

## C3. What Frappe 16 offers natively and where it falls short; external engine or ReBAC inside Frappe; cross-tenant consultants

### Takeaway
Frappe gives RBAC (Role, DocPerm, permlevels, if-owner), link-based User Permissions, document sharing, a list-filter hook and a document hook that can only deny. It has no native relation model with time windows or a single-holder invariant. Its sharing path can re-grant access after a hook denial. The product should model ReBAC inside Frappe through Plan Üyeliği and one central policy function. An external engine is not justified at today's scale with site-per-tenant.

### Cited Findings
- Frappe roles: a user holds several roles; each role defines actions on a DocType. DocPerm rows grant flags such as Read, Write, Create, Delete, Submit, Cancel, Amend, Report, Export, Import, Set User Permissions, Share, Print, Email. "If the user is owner" limits a rule to documents the user created. Permission levels group fields and apply separate roles to each level — [Frappe users and permissions](https://docs.frappe.io/framework/user/en/basics/users-and-permissions).
- Frappe User Permissions are per-user rules that filter documents by the value in a Link field — [Frappe users and permissions](https://docs.frappe.io/framework/user/en/basics/users-and-permissions).
- permission_query_conditions receives user (may be None) and returns a valid SQL WHERE fragment. "This hook will only affect the result of frappe.db.get_list method and not frappe.db.get_all" — [Frappe hooks](https://docs.frappe.io/framework/user/en/python-api/hooks).
- has_permission hook receives doc, user and permission_type and returns True or False; None falls back to default behaviour — [Frappe hooks](https://docs.frappe.io/framework/user/en/python-api/hooks).
- Frappe source (version-16 branch), permissions.py: the docstring says "Controllers can only deny permission, they can not explicitly grant any permission that wasn't" granted. In get_doc_permissions, a falsy controller result returns {ptype: 0} — [frappe/permissions.py, version-16](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/frappe/frappe/version-16/frappe/permissions.py).
- Same source: order for a document check is controller hooks, then role permissions, then if-owner overrides, then user permissions; sharing runs last. "if not perm and not ignore_share_permissions:" then "perm = false_if_not_shared()". So a DocShare grant can still give access after a controller denial. Sharing covers read, write, share, submit, email and print. Sharing is blocked for ptype "share" when disable_document_sharing is set — [frappe/permissions.py, version-16](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/frappe/frappe/version-16/frappe/permissions.py) (this behaviour was read by a summarizing fetch tool; confirm by reading the code and a test).
- Product fact: the repository already plans a list condition plus a record check, both written only by the Access module and bound through hooks.py; reads always use permission-checked paths; decisions only through workflow endpoints — R1 section 6.3.
- Product fact: each tenant has its own Frappe site and database; a central Kurum dizini tells the client which site to use — R1 sections 1 and 9.1.
- Zanzibar-style systems are built for very large scale (trillions of ACLs, millions of requests per second) — [USENIX, Pang et al. 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/pang).

Where Frappe falls short for this product (inference from the cited behaviour):
- Frappe roles are site-wide. "Karar sahibi of plan A but Ekip üyesi of plan B" cannot be a Frappe Role. It must be a relation row (Plan Üyeliği).
- User Permissions filter by link value but have no role-per-link, no time window and no single-holder rule.
- has_permission can only deny, so the plan check must start from a broad base role and narrow down.
- permission_query_conditions does not cover frappe.db.get_all, so any server code using get_all bypasses the list filter.
- DocShare can re-grant after a hook denial, so sharing must be switched off for plan data.
- Frappe Workflow states and transitions are role-based. They cannot by themselves express "only the current karar sahibi of this plan" or "reviewer is not the changer".

### Inferences
- Inference, recommendation: do not add OpenFGA, SpiceDB or a Cedar service now. Reasons: (1) each tenant site holds a small graph (users times plans); (2) an external store needs dual writes with Plan Üyeliği and a consistency story, while decisions must finalize in one database transaction under a plan lock (R1 section 7.2); (3) site-per-tenant would need one store or one namespace per tenant, plus new infrastructure for Hüseyin Cengiz to run; (4) offline clients only compute suggestions, the server decides (R1 section 7.1).
- Inference: model ReBAC inside Frappe. Plan Üyeliği is the relation tuple table (user, relation, plan, start, end, assigner). Add a small number of derived relations: task owner (from 3.2 rows), evidence author (from version rows), reviewer (from review fields).
- Inference: write one policy decision function in the Access module, for example authorize(user, action, resource, context), returning allow or deny and a reason. Every whitelisted endpoint and both hooks call it. Write policies as data plus small Python predicates, and run shared fixture tests, like the existing TypeScript and Python rule fixtures (R1 section 7.1).
- Inference: shape policies so they could move to Cedar later (principal, action, resource, context; permit and forbid). Cedar is a good fit for later because it is an embeddable library rather than a graph database. Whether a maintained Python binding exists was not checked.
- Inference: triggers to revisit an external engine: holding groups with nested companies and plans; consultant portfolio view needing cross-site relations; more than about 10 relation types; a need to explain decisions across several services.
- Inference, hardening: remove Share from all DocPerm rows of plan doctypes, set disable_document_sharing if no other app needs it, and add a regression test that a DocShare row does not grant access to a plan record. Ban frappe.db.get_all and ignore_permissions in plan modules except in reviewed system jobs, with a lint or test gate.
- Inference, cross-tenant consultants: keep one Frappe User per site. Do not share a database across tenants. The Kurum dizini can hold a consultant identity that lists the sites where the consultant has an active membership. A portfolio view would call each site with that site's own token and show only summaries each site allows. Each tenant's Kurum yöneticisi or karar sahibi grants and ends the consultant's membership locally. The consultant's partner agreement and KVKK roles need legal review.

### Gaps
- The v16 permission code was read through a summarizing fetch. A direct code read and a test on a v16 bench are needed to confirm the DocShare override and the hook order.
- Frappe v16 docs on Role Profile, Module Profile and applicable_for in User Permissions were not on the fetched pages.
- Frappe Workflow's support for condition expressions on transitions was not checked in this session.
- OpenFGA, SpiceDB and Cedar operational costs for a per-tenant setup were not researched.

## C4. Recommended concrete model: which part RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC, with plain rules and pseudo-policy

### Takeaway
Use three layers. Layer 1 is small RBAC in Frappe roles at site level. Layer 2 is ReBAC through Plan Üyeliği and derived relations. Layer 3 is ABAC and separation-of-duty checks in one server policy function. Default is deny. Forbid rules win over permit rules. Identity always comes from the token.

### Cited Findings
- All rule content below comes from repository facts in R1 sections 3.5, 5.1, 5.3, 6.3, 7.2, 7.3 and 9.3, and R3 sections 1 and 7, unless marked Inference.
- The deny-only nature of Frappe hooks and the DocShare fallback come from [frappe/permissions.py, version-16](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/frappe/frappe/version-16/frappe/permissions.py).
- The permit, forbid, when and unless structure follows Cedar's model — [Cedar terminology](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/overview/terminology.html).

Layer 1, RBAC (Frappe Role, site level):
- System Manager: operator only (Sistem yöneticisi). Not given to customer users.
- Kurum Yöneticisi: user and device management, sync settings. No plan data rights by this role alone.
- Plan Kullanıcısı (Inference, new base role): API and read access to plan doctypes, filtered by layers 2 and 3. No Share, Delete, Import or Export. No create or submit on decision doctypes.

Layer 2, ReBAC (relations inside each site):
- member(user, plan, role, start, end, assigner) from Plan Üyeliği.
- owner(user, task) from 3.2 rows.
- author(user, evidence_version) and author(user, observation) from creation fields.
- reviewer(user, limit_change) and second_opinion(user, decision).
- Invariant: for each plan, at most one active member row with role karar_sahibi at any time.

Layer 3, ABAC and separation of duty (policy function):
- Resource attributes: plan cluster, transition decided, gate locked, strategy approval valid, permit status, permit remaining, free budget, rule version.
- Environment attributes: device revoked, membership time window, client rule version.
- Actor attributes: is human user, is the system or AI service account.

Plain-language rules (examples):
1. A user sees a plan's records only while they are an active member of that plan.
2. Only the current karar sahibi of a plan records a gate, transition, strategy approval, strategy review or permit.
3. A stratej writes strategy steps only after the transition decision exists.
4. Nobody records "İlerle" on a locked execution gate.
5. A team member writes observations even if the permit is closed. The observation gives no spending right.
6. A movement on a closed permit is saved, but it goes to review. Only the karar sahibi accepts or rejects it.
7. The person who raised a limit cannot review that change.
8. The second opinion comes from a different member, with their own account.
9. An AI or system account never records a decision and never gives a second opinion.
10. A revoked device only sends pending changes to review and receives the delete command.
11. Plan records are never shared through Frappe document sharing.

Pseudo-policy (Cedar-like, Inference):

```
// default: deny everything not permitted

permit(principal, action == Action::"read", resource)
  when { member(principal, resource.plan).active_at(now) };

permit(principal, action in [Action::"record_gate", Action::"record_transition",
                             Action::"approve_strategy", Action::"record_review",
                             Action::"open_permit", Action::"close_permit",
                             Action::"review_movement"], resource)
  when { member(principal, resource.plan).role == "karar_sahibi"
         && member(principal, resource.plan).active_at(now) };

permit(principal, action == Action::"write_strategy_step", resource)
  when { member(principal, resource.plan).role in ["stratej", "karar_sahibi"]
         && resource.plan.transition_decided };

permit(principal, action in [Action::"add_observation", Action::"add_movement",
                             Action::"add_evidence_version"], resource)
  when { member(principal, resource.plan).role in ["ekip_uyesi", "karar_sahibi", "stratej"] };

permit(principal, action == Action::"give_second_opinion", resource)
  when { member(principal, resource.plan).role in ["danisman", "ekip_uyesi"] };

forbid(principal, action == Action::"record_gate", resource)
  when { resource.gate.locked && resource.choice == "Ilerle" };

forbid(principal, action == Action::"give_second_opinion", resource)
  when { principal == resource.decision.karar_sahibi };

forbid(principal, action == Action::"review_limit_change", resource)
  when { principal == resource.changed_by };

forbid(principal, action, resource)
  when { principal.is_service_account
         && action in [Action::"record_gate", Action::"record_transition",
                       Action::"approve_strategy", Action::"record_review",
                       Action::"open_permit", Action::"give_second_opinion"] };

forbid(principal, action, resource)
  when { context.device.revoked
         && !(action in [Action::"send_pending_to_review", Action::"receive_wipe"]) };

forbid(principal, action == Action::"share", resource)
  when { resource.is_plan_record };
```

Mapping to Frappe enforcement points:
- permission_query_conditions for every plan doctype: active membership filter on Plan Üyeliği (repository already plans this, R1 section 6.3).
- has_permission hook for every plan doctype: calls the policy function; can only deny, which fits default deny after a broad base role.
- Whitelisted workflow endpoints for decisions: call the policy function, then the rule engine, then write under the plan lock (R1 section 7.2).
- before_submit and validate: repeat critical checks as a second layer (R1 section 6.3).
- DocPerm: no Share for plan doctypes; no create or submit for decision doctypes.

### Inferences
- Inference: Delegation for karar sahibi should not exist for recording decisions. The rule "one karar sahibi per plan" already allows a clean handover by a new time-bounded assignment. If a delegate is needed, limit it to reading and preparing drafts, with an end date, and never to recording.
- Inference: Danışman as second-opinion giver is a ReBAC relation per plan. The same person may be Danışman in many tenant sites through separate site accounts.
- Inference: The policy function should return a reason code. The UI can show "Only the plan's karar sahibi can record this decision." This supports plain-language error messages.
- Inference: Keep policy tests as shared fixtures run by CI, like the existing TypeScript and Python rule fixtures. A failing authorization fixture should stop the release.

### Gaps
- Product decisions needed: delegate rule, whether Ekip üyesi may give second opinion on limit increases (repository says Ekip üyesi "may give second opinion", R1 section 3.5), data classification, and self-review rule for movements in single-member plans.
- The pseudo-policy is illustrative. It was not executed in Cedar or any engine.
