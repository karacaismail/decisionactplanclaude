## 2. Ansoff: turbulence levels, strategic aggressiveness and capability responsiveness

### Takeaway
Ansoff and McDonnell (1990) name five turbulence levels (Repetitive, Expanding, Changing, Discontinuous, Surprising), five matching aggressiveness levels (Stable, Reactive, Anticipatory, Entrepreneurial, Creative) and five capability responsiveness levels (Custodial, Production, Marketing, Strategic, Flexible). Performance potential is optimal when aggressiveness matches turbulence, capability responsiveness matches aggressiveness, and capability components support one another.

### Cited Findings
- Turbulence scale (Figure 2.1.1, ch. 2.1, around p. 31-32): levels labelled Repetitive, Expanding ("Slow Incremental"), Changing ("Fast Incremental"), Discontinuous ("Discontinuous Predictable"), Surprising ("Discontinuous Unpredictable"). In the running text, Ansoff also uses the adjective "surpriseful" for level 5 environments [V1-s] — [Ansoff and McDonnell 1990, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780134518817)
- Strategic aggressiveness (same figure family): Stable "based on precedents"; Reactive, incremental, "based on experience"; Anticipatory, incremental, "based on extrapolation"; Entrepreneurial, discontinuous, based on expected futures; Creative, discontinuous, "based on creativity" [V1-s] — same source
- Responsiveness of capability: Custodial "suppresses change"; Production "adapts to change"; Marketing "seeks familiar change"; Strategic "seeks new change"; Flexible "seeks novel change". A second figure in the book labels the top level "Creative/flexible" [V1-s] — same source
- Strategic success hypothesis, p. 31: a firm's "performance potential is optimum" when three conditions are met: (1) aggressiveness of strategic behaviour matches environmental turbulence; (2) responsiveness of capability "matches the aggressiveness" of strategy; (3) the components of capability are "supportive of one another" [V1-s] — same source
- The 2nd edition states the hypothesis was empirically validated by "seven extensive research studies" in different industries [V1-s] — same source
- Management-system history in the book: a first system when change was slow; "Management by extrapolation, when change accelerated, but the future could be predicted by extrapolation of the past"; then "Management by anticipation". A figure pairs "Forecastable by extrapolation" with operations budgeting, capital budgeting, management by objectives and "Long range planning", and lists "issue management" and "Surprise management" toward higher turbulence levels [V1-s] — same source
- Diagnostic procedure in the book: estimate the future turbulence level of each strategic business area (SBA) and group SBAs into turbulence categories [V1-s] — same source
- Label conflict resolved: Kipley et al. (2012) use "exploring" for level-4 behaviour; the 1990 book uses "Entrepreneurial" for level-4 aggressiveness. Earlier Ansoff texts used other labels; for Ansoff and McDonnell 1990 the labels above apply [V1-s vs V3] — [Kipley et al. 2012](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/17515631211286083/full/html)

### Inferences
- Ansoff's unit of analysis is the strategic business area, not the whole firm. For a pre-assessment, the turbulence question fits a single business line or decision, which aligns with the existing Courtney-based İ3 proposal.
- Ansoff gives a direct "type of planning" signal: extrapolative long-range planning suits levels 1-3; at levels 4-5 it is inadequate and anticipatory or flexible systems are needed.

### Gaps
- Exact page numbers for the aggressiveness and capability figures were not shown in the search output; Figure 2.1.1 and the hypothesis are on or near p. 31-32.

## 3. Gersick, Davis, McCollom Hampton and Lansberg 1997: stage names on three axes

### Takeaway
All stage names are now confirmed from the book. Ownership: Controlling Owner, Sibling Partnership, Cousin Consortium. Family: Young Business Family, Entering the Business, Working Together, Passing the Baton. Business: Start-Up, Expansion/Formalization, Maturity. "Strategic planning" is named as a key challenge of the Expansion/Formalization stage.

### Cited Findings
- Family axis: "We call these stages Young Business Family, Entering the Business, Working Together, and Passing the Baton" (figure I-5, Introduction; detailed in ch. 2, figure 2-1) [V1-s] — [Gersick et al. 1997, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/generationtogene00gers)
- Ownership axis: ownership development is "well captured in three stages: Controlling Owner companies, Sibling Partnerships, and Cousin Consortium companies" (ch. 1). The book notes a Controlling Owner business may pass to one child (Controlling Owner to Controlling Owner) and may skip stages [V1-s] — same source
- Business axis: three stages, "Start-Up, Expansion/Formalization, and Maturity" (figure 3-1, ch. 3; also figure 1-6) [V1-s] — same source
- Start-Up key challenges begin with "Survival (market entry, business planning" (the search hit ends there) [V1-s] — same source, ch. 3
- Expansion/Formalization key challenges: evolving the owner-manager role and professionalizing the business; "Strategic planning"; organizational systems and policies; "Cash management" [V1-s] — same source, ch. 3
- Maturity key challenges list starts with "Strategic refocus" and "Management and ownership commitment"; the text links strategic refocus to "the business's needs for reinvestment" and says it "typically requires investment" [V1-s] — same source, ch. 3
- Sibling Partnership firms: in a case, the siblings "have had to attend seriously to strategic planning"; the book links formalization with the Sibling Partnership stage [V1-s] — same source

### Inferences
- Gersick et al. give a stage-based, authority-backed reason for planning need: the business axis, not the family axis, carries the explicit "strategic planning" challenge (Expansion/Formalization). İ6 (generation, two generations together) captures the family and ownership axes, but not the business axis; a business-stage question would complete it.

## 4. Carlock and Ward 2001 and 2010: parallel planning and the link between strategy and family commitment

### Takeaway
Carlock and Ward's Parallel Planning Process (PPP) runs family planning and business planning side by side. Family Commitment and management's Strategic Commitment are explored in parallel and joined through a "Strategic Fit". The family's investment decision depends on the business's Strategic Potential combined with Family Commitment, giving six scenarios: sell, harvest, hold, reformulate, renew, regenerate.

### Cited Findings
- The book "proposes a Parallel Planning Process (PPP)"; strategic planning using the PPP is described as "very different" from conventional planning (ch. 2, around pp. 32-39) [V1-s] — [Carlock and Ward 2001, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/strategicplannin00carl_0)
- Family business planning has "traditionally centered on two issues - estate planning and succession"; the next sentence begins "These goals are far ..." (hit ends there; the earlier V3 summaries read it as "too narrow") (ch. 1) [V1-s] — same source
- Chapter 3 "Securing Family Commitment" (contents: Understanding p. 52, Exploring p. 55): "Family Commitment is what makes a family business" a family business [V1-s] — same source
- PPP joins the family's Family Commitment and management's Strategic Commitment; "the Strategic Fit is based on the Business Strategy Plan" (ch. 2; sections 2.7 "Thinking about strategic commitment" and 2.8) [V1-s] — same source
- The family enterprise continuity plan is the family-side output (section 2.9; Appendix E "Reardon Family Enterprise Continuity Plan", p. 250) [V1-s] — same source
- Investment link: family investment commitment and business Strategic Potential determine six scenarios, "sell, harvest, hold, reformulate, renew, and regenerate". The book's table pairs higher family commitment with renew, reformulate or regenerate (investment, marked + to +++) and lower commitment with harvest, hold or sell [V1-s; the column reading of the table is my reading of the extracted table text] — same source
- Businesses with high Family Commitment "can optimize their growth"; with low commitment and weak potential the family "should harvest or sell the business" [V1-s] — same source
- The book cites a study in which problems in the successor generation "were related to inadequate estate planning" [V1-s] — same source
- 2010 update: *When Family Businesses Are Best* keeps the PPP; Table 7.1 presents "investment scenarios based on different combinations of Strategic Potential and Family Commitment"; Figure 4.6 lists four investment strategies: invest, hold, harvest, sell; Figure 6.3 is a strategic potential model [V1-s] — [Carlock and Ward 2010, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/whenfamilybusine0000carl)

### Inferences
- H2 and H3 now rest on a primary source: Carlock and Ward make family commitment a precondition for the investment decision. A low-commitment family is, in their logic, a harvest or sell case, not a growth-planning case. That is a "not now" or "different product" signal.

## 5. Reeves, Haanaes and Sinha 2015: harshness and the renewal approach

### Takeaway
The book defines a harsh environment by cause: a protracted mismatch between the firm's approach to strategy and its environment, or an acute external or internal shock, threatening viability or survival. Renewal has two phases: react (or anticipate) and economize, then grow; most failed renewals stopped after cost cutting.

### Cited Findings
- Harshness is one of three palette axes with unpredictability and malleability; it "can require companies to economize and focus on short-term survival" (ch. 1) [V1-s] — [Reeves, Haanaes and Sinha 2015, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yourstrategyneed0000reev)
- Renewal "aims to restore the vitality and competitiveness of a firm when it is operating in a harsh environment"; such a challenge can be caused by a protracted mismatch between the firm's approach to "strategy and its environment" or by an acute external or internal "shock" (ch. 1 and ch. 6) [V1-s] — same source
- Self-diagnosis line in ch. 1: "Renewal: My resources are severely constrained" [V1-s] — same source
- Warning signs: declining growth, profitability, free cash flows "or reductions in available capital are all signs that the long-term survival of the firm may be at risk" (ch. 6) [V1-s] — same source
- Renewal process: "react (or anticipate), economize, grow"; the firm must first react to the "deteriorating environment as early as possible", then economize, then grow (ch. 6, p. 152 region) [V1-s] — same source
- Failures occurred in "firms that never went beyond the first phase of cost cutting"; a firm must initiate both the first phase of economizing and the second of growth (ch. 6) [V1-s] — same source
- Renewal is a "prelude to adopting one of the other" approaches (ch. 1, figure 1-9) [V1-s] — same source
- Correction stands: five approaches (classical, adaptive, visionary, shaping, renewal) plus ambidexterity ("Be Polychromatic", ch. 7) as combination [V1-s] — same source

### Inferences
- Z1 (months of cash) and Z2 (restructuring) now map to Reeves' renewal warning signs. The book still gives no numeric cash threshold; the threshold remains a product decision.

### Gaps
- No numeric harshness threshold exists in the text found.

## 6. Zook and Allen 2016: three crises and their timing

### Takeaway
The three crises are overload, stall-out and free fall. Timing is by phase, not by age or revenue: overload hits insurgents scaling up; stall-out hits companies that have scaled; free fall can happen at any point.

### Cited Findings
- Overload "refers to the internal dysfunction and loss of external momentum" that management faces as growth takes the company to "the next level of the game" (ch. 2-3, figure 3-1) [V1-s] — [Zook and Allen 2016, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/foundersmentalit0000zook)
- "Stall-out hits companies that have successfully scaled and are now struggling with the challenges of complexity" (ch. 4) [V1-s] — same source
- The authors estimate that "two-thirds of maturing companies" will find themselves encountering a growth crisis of this kind (hit text ends before the noun); of firms moving from insurgency to incumbency in a recent fifteen-year period, two-thirds faced stall-out [V1-s] — same source
- "Nearly nine in ten" stall-out companies "lose over half of their market value" [V1-s] — same source

## 7. McKinsey power-curve odds

### Takeaway
Confirmed from the McKinsey Quarterly article: 8% base odds over ten years for a mid-curve firm; one or two big moves raise this to 17%; three to 47%. The data are the world's 2,393 largest corporations.

### Cited Findings
- Database: "the world's 2,393 largest corporations", 2010-2014 averages (p. 4); top quintile captures "nearly 90 percent of the economic profit created" (p. 4) [V1] — [McKinsey Quarterly, February 2018](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategy-to-beat-the-odds)
- Odds of moving from the middle quintiles to the top quintile over ten years are 8 percent, "just 1 in 12 companies" (p. 6) [V1] — same source
- Pulling one or two of five levers "more than doubles their odds of rising into the top quintile, from 8 percent to 17 percent. Three big moves boost these odds to 47 percent" (p. 9) [V1] — same source
- Endowment explains about 30% and trends about 25% of the odds of moving (p. 6) [V1] — same source

### Inferences
- These are large-company figures. Using them for Turkish SMEs needs an explicit warning.

### Gaps
- None.

## 10. Measurement instruments: item lists and licensing

### Takeaway
Primary item lists were obtained for Aylan and Koç 2017 (7 strategic-planning items), Karabulut 2008 (4 yes/no planning criteria), Zor 2018 (uses Henri 2006 six-item scale) and Bağatır and Kasımoğlu 2024 (14 rated growth strategies, not a stage scale). Boyd and Reuning-Elliott, Bracker and Pearson and Rue and Ibrahim stay paywalled. An open-access substitute with full activity wording is Watts and Ormsby (Journal of Small Business Strategy).

### Cited Findings
- Aylan and Koç 2017, "Stratejik planlama süreci" factor, items 23-29: written mission; written mission known by all; written vision; written vision known by all; clear general and specific goals; plans always long-term; good goal alignment across units and levels (p. 577). Subscale alpha .85; whole scale .98, 48 items, 9 factors (p. 582). Sample: 112 usable forms from 200 hotel employees in Karaman (p. 574) [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/459319)
- Karabulut 2008 planning criteria: written strategic plan (yes/no); person or unit responsible; tactical plans by department managers; action plans by employees (pp. 657-658) [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3634)
- Zor 2018: planning and control measured with Henri (2006) six statements on a 7-point Likert scale after a yes/no budget question; professional manager coded 0/1 (pp. 190-191) [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/453137)
- Bağatır and Kasımoğlu 2024: survey with three parts; 14 growth strategies (e.g., product development, market entry, merger, acquisition, competitive strategies) rated for importance on a 5-point scale; "factor analysis not been performed"; alpha 0.861 (pp. 374-375). Licence: Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 (p. 1) [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3990547)
- Watts and Ormsby, "The Effect of Operational and Strategic Planning on Small Firm Performance", Journal of Small Business Strategy: strategic-planning clusters drawn from Hofer and Schendel: assess strategic position; identify opportunities and threats; identify resources and skills; identify strategic issues; item examples "Assess the relative competitive position", "Identify changes in actions of competitors". Comprehensiveness measured after Fredrickson (group, analysis, expense, diversity, options). Validity: expert panel and owner interviews. Sample: small banks, 48.8% response [V1, partial read] — [JSBS PDF](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/jsbs/jsbs-227.pdf)
- Boyd and Reuning-Elliott 1998: SMJ 19(2):181-192; multiple-indicator measure validated on two samples [V2] — [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/a-measurement-model-of-strategic-planning/). A 2022 review notes the paper found over three-quarters of 1982-1993 studies focused on economic benefits [V1 of the review] — [Amoo et al. 2022, Leeds Beckett](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/8119/1/OverHalfACenturyOfStrategicPlanningPerformanceResearchWhatHaveWeBeenMissingAM-AMOO.pdf)
- Bracker and Pearson 1986: abstract compares firms with "structured, strategic plans" and those without [V2] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v7y1986i6p503-522.html)

### Inferences
- For Turkish wording, Aylan and Koç's 7 items remain the closest validated set, but they measure planning practice from an employee view, not planning need. Bağatır and Kasımoğlu should be removed from the instrument list.
- Karabulut's four yes/no criteria are a simple, Turkish-tested way to place a firm on a planning-practice ladder, and they map to Gluck's phases.

### Gaps
- Routes tried for Boyd and Reuning-Elliott, Bracker and Pearson, Rue and Ibrahim: Wiley/SMJ and JSBM pages (paywalled), Semantic Scholar API (rate-limited, 429), OpenAlex API (budget-limited), CORE download (corrupt stream), Strathprints and Maynooth repositories, web search for citing papers with item lists. Item wording not reached.
- Licence of Aylan and Koç 2017 (Gazi İİBF Dergisi) and Watts and Ormsby (JSBS) was not confirmed on the journal pages. No Turkish-validated strategic planning scale with a confirmed permissive (commercial-use) licence was found.

## 11. Turkish studies: full re-reads and new evidence

### Takeaway
Full re-reads confirm Karabulut 2008 and Zor 2018 and add detail; Peksaygılı and Tutan 2015 give a 47% written-plan rate. PwC Türkiye 2023 gives 85% clear purpose, 71% some governance policy and only 50% family alignment on direction (n=34). No Turkey-specific 2018-2019 PwC plan figure exists in sources found. Glaister et al. remain abstract-level.

### Cited Findings
**Karabulut 2008 (full re-read)** [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3634)
- Method: ISO member database, 2,314 textile firms with contact data; 200 randomly chosen general managers e-mailed in 2008 over two months; 96 usable answers (48%); 7 excluded as non-family or 250+ staff; SPSS frequencies and chi-square; sampling error 8.22% (p. 657, p. 659)
- Table 4 (p. 660): general manager from the family 88.4% (84 of 95). Table 3: 68.8% have no non-family board member beyond legal requirement
- Table 8 (p. 661): written strategic plan yes 16.0% (15), no 84.0% (79), n=94
- Table 9 (p. 662): responsible for strategic planning: general manager 80.7%, planning department 8.4%, planning manager 6.0%, department managers 2.4%, other 2.4% (n=83)
- Table 10 (p. 662): planning horizon 1-3 years 85.0%, 4-5 years 11.3%, 6-10 years 2.5%, 11-15 years 1.3% (n=80)
- Table 11-12 (pp. 662-663): tactical plans by department managers 63.9%; employees' own action plans 52.9%
- Table 17 (p. 664): no leadership development programme 87.1%
- Conclusions (p. 671): significant chi-square links were found between institutionalisation criteria (family purchasing head, family finance head, non-family board members) and tactical plans, action plans, manuals, charts and delegation. The summary lists no significant link for the written strategic plan.

**Zor 2018 (full re-read)** [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/453137)
- Sample: Marmara region SMEs with more than 10 staff (micro excluded); 591 contacted, 200 agreed (34%), 136 reported planning and control activities (p. 189)
- Respondents: accounting managers or the person responsible for accounting (p. 189)
- Table 1 (p. 192): share using planning and control by size: 10-19 staff 62%, 20-49 71%, 50-99 77%, 100-149 67%, 150-199 61%, 200-249 72%; total 68%
- Results (p. 193): path coefficients to the extent of planning and control: professional manager 0.238, competition 0.203, size 0.164; R² 20%. Planning and control to performance 0.436; R² 26%
- Limits stated by the author: small sample cannot represent Turkish SMEs (p. 193)

**Peksaygılı and Tutan 2015 (full re-read)** [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/179567)
- Sample: 44 active first- and second-generation owners in EGOD; 60 questions; 60% first generation, 40% second; about two-thirds founded in the 1980s-1990s; 82% employ 6-49, 11% 50-249, 7% 1-5 (p. 6436)
- Table 2 (p. 6437): mission, vision and objectives defined 80%; written strategic plan 47% yes, 53% no; clear job descriptions 68%; organisation chart 66%; grooming a successor 59%; top-management rules such as a family constitution 39%
- Table 3 (p. 6438): "institutionalisation is necessary" 75% agree; "we need a succession plan" 33% agree, 28% undecided, 39% disagree; "family constitution needed" 39% agree

**PwC Family Business Survey 2023, Türkiye report** [V1] — [PwC Türkiye PDF](https://www.pwc.com.tr/tr/Hizmetlerimiz/aile-sirketleri/yayinlar/2023/pdf/family-business-survey-2023.pdf)

- A large share of Turkish boards include no non-family members (slide 8)
- The 2023 Türkiye report has no strategic-plan question.

**Strategy& / PwC Türkiye CSO study (2019)** [V1] — [Strategy& PDF](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/tr/tr/pdf/stratejiden-sorumlu-ust-duzey-yonetici-calismasi.pdf)
- 187 CSOs; 82% say strategic planning is a "very important" part of their job; 40% say the process is too bureaucratic and tends to miss market change; 65% say priorities are not very clear; 25% "very successful" at creating value (pp. 2, 7-8). ### Inferences
- Two Turkish family-SME samples give 16% (2008, Istanbul textiles) and 47% (2015, Aegean automotive) written strategic plans. A cautious best estimate for Turkish family SMEs is "a minority to about half have a written plan"; neither study is representative.
- Zor's size table shows that the presence of budget-based planning does not rise steadily with size between 10 and 249 staff; the size effect is on the extent of use. So "size" is a weak signal for presence and a moderate one for depth.
- PwC Türkiye 2023: only half report family alignment on direction. This supports Carlock and Ward's family-commitment precondition as a real readiness issue in Turkish family firms.
