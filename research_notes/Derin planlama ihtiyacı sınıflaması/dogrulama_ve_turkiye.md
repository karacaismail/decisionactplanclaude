## 1. Verification of the flagged international authorities

### Takeaway
Seven of the eleven flagged items are now V1 (Gluck et al., Courtney et al., Greiner, Churchill and Lewis, Drucker 1994, Mintzberg 1994, PwC global 2018 data). Three earlier statements must be corrected: Gluck et al. say skipping a phase "doesn't appear possible" (softer than "cannot") and also say phase one can be enough; BCG's book has five approaches plus "ambidexterity" as a way of combining them; Greiner's sixth phase is a 1998 speculation, not a model phase. Ansoff, Gersick et al. and Carlock and Ward remain V3.

### Cited Findings

**Gluck, Kaufman and Walleck 1980 (HBR 58(4), July-August 1980; republished by McKinsey Quarterly as "Thinking strategically", 1 June 2000)** [V1]
- Based on a McKinsey study of formal planning in corporations, planning "routinely progresses through four discrete phases": (1) financial planning, (2) forecast-based planning, (3) externally oriented planning, (4) strategic management. Section: introduction before "Phase one" — [McKinsey Quarterly republication](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically) (read via [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically))
- Skipping: the authors write that it "doesn't appear possible to skip a step", because each phase builds attitudes and capabilities needed later. This is softer than the earlier report's "cannot skip a phase". Same section — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- Same paragraph: many companies succeeded without moving beyond rudimentary levels; some large, successful firms are still in forecast-based planning; whether they are lagging or responding appropriately to a slowly changing environment "must be determined on a case-by-case basis" — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- Phase one is the annual budget. In "well over half" of the companies studied, formal planning was still at this phase. Phase-one firms do have strategies; their quality depends on the CEO's and top team's entrepreneurial vigour. If top managers know the competition and their own cost structures, "there may be little advantage to formal strategic planning"; ad hoc task-force studies and communication of the strategy "may suffice". Section "Phase one: Financial planning" — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- Trigger to phase two: most large enterprises are too complex for an implicit strategy; firms learn phase one's limits when the treasurer struggles to estimate capital needs and financing trade-offs on a one-year budget. Phase two then fails because extrapolations fail, which leads to "issue orientation". Section "Phase two" — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- Phase three turns analysis outward (customers, competitors, suppliers); plans are dynamic, adaptive, often several options. Phase four melds planning and everyday management; "no more than a few" companies, mainly diversified multinationals, had reached it. Sections "Phase three" and "Phase Four" — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- The article notes formal planning is only one of at least three sources of sound strategy (the others: strategic thinking, opportunistic decision making) — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- The text is "adapted from a McKinsey staff paper dated October 1978" — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)

**Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie 1997 (HBR November-December 1997; adapted in McKinsey Quarterly 2000, pp. 81-90)** [V1]
- "Residual uncertainty" is what remains after the best possible analysis; it falls into four levels. Section "Four levels of uncertainty", p. 82 — [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) (read via [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2021/https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf))
- Level 1 "A clear enough future": a single forecast is precise enough; the standard toolkit (market research, competitor cost analysis, value chain, five forces, DCF) works. Level 2 "Alternative futures": a few discrete scenarios; example: firms facing major regulatory or legislative change. Level 3 "A range of futures": a range defined by a few variables, no natural scenarios; example: emerging industries or entering new geographic markets. Level 4 "True ambiguity": not even a range can be identified; such situations are rare and transitional. pp. 82-85 — [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf)
- Three postures: shapers drive the industry to a new structure; adapters take the structure as given and react; "reserving the right to play" is a special form of adaptation, relevant only in levels 2 to 4, with incremental investments that let the firm wait until uncertainty falls. Three move types: big bets, options, no-regrets moves. Sidebar, p. 86 — [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf)
- In predictable (level 1) environments "most companies are adapters"; shaping at level 1 is "risky and rare". p. 85-87 — [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf)
- Level 4 options should be re-evaluated whenever key uncertainties clarify "and at least every six months". p. 90 — [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf)
- Classification note: the levels describe the uncertainty around a strategic decision, not the firm as a whole. The article says the framework helps judge "which analytic tools can and can't help" at each level. p. 90 — same source.

**Reeves, Haanaes and Sinha 2015, *Your Strategy Needs a Strategy* (HBR Press)** [V2]
- Publisher-side description: the environment is assessed on predictability, malleability and harshness ("how unpredictable it is, how much power you have to change it, and how harsh it is"). Named approaches: Be Big, Be First, Be Fast, Be the Orchestrator, Be Viable — [IMD book page](https://www.imd.org/research-knowledge/books/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- BCG's Strategy Palette page lists five approaches (classical, adaptive, visionary, shaping, renewal) and, separately, "ambidexterity", defined as applying multiple approaches "either concurrently or successively". Renewal applies in "a harsh environment" — [BCG Strategy Palette](https://www.bcg.com/publications/collections/your-strategy-needs-strategy/intro.aspx)
- Correction to the earlier report: "six approaches" is accurate only if ambidexterity is counted. BCG presents it as combining approaches, not as a sixth environment type. The formal definition of harshness was not found in an open page (V3 summaries describe it as threat to viability) — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy-summary-martin-reeves-knut-haanaes-and-janmejaya-sinha) [V3]

**Churchill and Lewis 1983, "The Five Stages of Small Business Growth" (HBR 61(3), May-June 1983)** [V1]
- Framing example: a 6-month-old, 20-person firm needs cash-flow planning most; a 30-year-old, 100-person manufacturer needs strategic planning and budgeting for coordination and control. Introduction — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1983/05/the-five-stages-of-small-business-growth) (read via [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.growthforce.com/hubfs/The_Five_Stages_of_Small_Business_Growth.pdf))
- Each stage is described by five management factors: managerial style, organizational structure, extent of formal systems, major strategic goals, owner's involvement — same source, section "Developing a Small Business Framework"
- Stage I Existence: systems and formal planning "minimal to nonexistent". Stage II Survival: "formal planning is, at best, cash forecasting". Same source, stage sections.
- Stage III-D (Success-Disengagement): basic financial, marketing, production systems; "planning in the form of operational budgets supports functional delegation"; the strategy is essentially to maintain the status quo; a firm can stay here indefinitely. Same source.
- Stage III-G (Success-Growth): operational planning still in budgets, but "strategic planning is extensive and deeply involves the owner". Same source.
- Stage IV Take-off: decentralized, partly divisionalized; "both operational and strategic planning are being done and involve specific managers". Same source.
- Stage V Resource Maturity: the firm uses budgets, strategic planning, management by objectives and standard costs to professionalize, and "has the staff and financial resources to engage in detailed operational and strategic planning". Same source.
- Owner factors: goals, operational abilities, managerial ability and willingness to delegate, strategic abilities. Their importance changes from stage to stage. Same source.
- Evidence base: 83 questionnaire responses from 110 owners and managers of small firms with sales of 1 to 35 million US dollars; the data showed the "grow-or-fail" assumption was invalid, as some firms plateaued for 5 to 80 years. Same source.

**Greiner 1972, "Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow" (HBR July-August 1972; republished HBR May-June 1998 with commentary)** [V1]
- Five phases and their ending crises: Creativity, ending in a crisis of leadership; Direction, ending in a crisis of autonomy; Delegation, ending in a crisis of control; Coordination, ending in a red-tape crisis; Collaboration, ending in a crisis Greiner first marked "?" — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1998/05/evolution-and-revolution-as-organizations-grow) (read via [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://naturalsciences.ucmerced.edu/sites/g/files/ufvvjh1296/f/page/documents/evolution_and_revolution_as_organizations_grow.pdf))
- Phase 2 Direction introduces "incentives, budgets, and work standards". Phase 4 Coordination brings formal systems, product groups and "formal planning procedures are established and intensively reviewed"; these systems later exceed their usefulness and cause the red-tape crisis. Same source.
- Evolutionary periods typically last "four to eight years". Firms in fast-growing industries pass through all five phases faster; firms in slow-growing industries meet "only two or three phases over many years". Same source.
- Each phase is "at once a result of the previous phase and a cause for the next"; a firm in a Phase 2 autonomy crisis "cannot return to directive management". Same source.
- 1998 commentary: Greiner now thinks the Phase 5 crisis is realising there is no internal solution for growth; "a sixth phase may be evolving" around extra-organizational solutions (holding company, network). This is speculation, not a sixth model phase. Same source, commentary "Revolution Is Still Inevitable".

**Drucker 1994, "The Theory of the Business" (HBR September-October 1994)** [V1]
- "Preventive care" has two measures. The first is "abandonment": every three years, the organization should challenge every product, service, policy and distribution channel with the question "If we were not in it already, would we be going into it now?" The second is to study what goes on outside, especially non-customers. p. 102 (page before the p. 103 header) — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1994/09/the-theory-of-the-business)
- Warning signs that the theory is obsolete: attaining the original objectives ("cause for new thinking"); rapid growth (any organization that "doubles or triples in size within a fairly short period of time has necessarily outgrown its theory"); unexpected success, own or a competitor's; unexpected failure, own or a competitor's. p. 103 — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1994/09/the-theory-of-the-business)
- The "every three years" interval, flagged as unverified earlier, is now confirmed [V1].

**Mintzberg 1994, "Rethinking Strategic Planning Part II: New Roles for Planners" (Long Range Planning 27(3):22-30)** [V1]
- What is called strategic planning "in reality is strategic programming". In the absence of strategy "there is no reason to engage in formalized strategic planning"; given viable, stable strategies, planning's role is to programme (implement) them. Section "Programming" — [mintzberg.org PDF](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf)
- Conditions: programming makes sense when the world is expected to hold still or change predictably; contexts relatively stable or under the firm's control, typically mature industries; the organization usually rather large, often capital intensive, with an elaborated structure and simple, tightly coupled operations. Such firms tend to be "machine bureaucracy" (airlines, retail banking, much mass production, the post office). "In other circumstances, strategic programming can do an organization harm" by pre-empting the flexibility needed to learn. Same section, PDF p. 3 — same source.
- Planners' other roles named in the same paper: plans as communication media and control devices; "right-handed" planners for machine bureaucracies and "left-handed" planners for adhocracies. Same source.
- Correction: the PDF has a text layer; the earlier report's note that it was unreadable no longer applies. The earlier V3 summary is now confirmed as accurate.

**Ansoff, turbulence levels (Ansoff and McDonnell 1990, *Implanting Strategic Management*, 2nd ed.)** [V3]
- Five turbulence levels from placid to unpredictable; the success formula says both strategic aggressiveness and capability must match turbulence; at levels 4 and 5 extrapolation is dangerous — [ANZAM 2013 paper](https://www.anzam.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf-manager/16_ANZAM-2013-016.PDF)
- Level 1 described as "repetitive" and level 5 as creative and "surpriseful"; level 4 called "exploring" in one paper — [Kipley et al. 2012, Business Strategy Series 13(6)](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/17515631211286083/full/html) [V3, search summary]
- The level names for 2 to 4 and the management-mode column were not confirmed from Ansoff's text in this session.

**Gersick, Davis, Hampton and Lansberg 1997, *Generation to Generation* (HBS Press)** [V3]
- Three developmental axes (ownership, family, business). Ownership stages: Controlling Owner, Sibling Partnership, Cousin Consortium. Equal inheritance tends to move firms to Sibling Partnership — [CFEG summary](https://cfeg.com/about/thought-leadership-stages); [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/family-business/family-business-lifecycle)
- A secondary source reports four family-axis and three business-axis stages; names not confirmed — search summary only. The library table of contents page blocked access.

**PwC Global Family Business Survey 2018 (reported in the 2019 Irish report)** [V1]
- Fieldwork late 2018; "almost 3,000 firms in 53 territories"; Irish sample almost 130. p. 2 — [PwC 2019 Irish Family Business Report](https://aib.ie/content/dam/frontdoor/business/docs/my-business-is/family-business/pwc-2019-irish-family-business-report.pdf) (read via [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://aib.ie/content/dam/frontdoor/business/docs/my-business-is/family-business/pwc-2019-irish-family-business-report.pdf))

Ireland: 36% / 40% / 24%. So 51% globally and 64% in Ireland lack a formalised plan. p. 20 — same source
- In Ireland, 41% of double-digit growth firms had a fully costed, formalised, documented plan versus 34% of lower-growth firms. PwC says mid-term planning (3 to 5 years) is "often the biggest piece of the puzzle missing". p. 20 — same source
- Turkey: no Turkey-specific 2018 or 2019 strategic plan figure was found. The PwC Turkey 2023 report PDF did not download. A search summary reports that in the 2023 survey, 89% of Turkish family firms (79% global) said they have a clear mission, and 71% (81% global) have some governance policy [V3] — [PwC Türkiye 11. Küresel Aile Şirketleri Araştırması](https://www.pwc.com.tr/tr/hizmetlerimiz/aile-sirketleri/yayinlar/pwc-11-kuresel-aile-sirketleri-arastirmasi.html)

**Related item found while checking (stage and planning type)** [V3]
- Lumpkin and Dess 1995 (Academy of Management Journal): a "simplistic strategy-making process" was positively associated with performance in early stages but detrimental as organizations grow and mature. Quoted in [Hodges and Kent 2006/2007, Journal of Small Business Strategy 17(2), p. 76](https://libjournals.mtsu.edu/index.php/jsbs/article/view/73)

### Inferences
- The verified texts support a "stage and trigger" view rather than a fixed firm typology. Gluck et al. even allow that phase one can be adequate when top managers know competitors and costs.
- Churchill and Lewis give the clearest verified link between stage and planning form: cash forecasting (Survival), operational budgets (III-D), extensive owner-led strategic planning (III-G), manager-involved strategic planning (IV).
- Greiner places formal planning procedures as the answer to the crisis of control, and warns that the same systems later cause red tape. A product that adds planning procedures should expect this second effect.

### Gaps
- Ansoff level names 2 to 4 and the matching management modes are not confirmed from Ansoff's own text.
- Gersick et al. family-axis and business-axis stage names, and Carlock and Ward's matching of strategy to family commitment, remain unverified.
- BCG's formal definition of "harshness" was not read in a primary text.
- Exact HBR page ranges for Gluck 1980, Churchill-Lewis 1983 and Greiner 1972 were not checked; only section names are given.

## 2. Turkish evidence: planning practice in Turkish SMEs and family firms, stage models, institutionalisation

### Takeaway
Turkish evidence is thin but consistent. One family-SME study found 84% had no written strategic plan and planning sat with the general manager. Larger listed or ISO 500 firms show a strong planning-performance link. Size, competitive intensity and a professional manager go with more planning and control. Mission, vision and strategic plans appear on the agenda when the second generation enters. No Turkish study applying Churchill-Lewis directly was found, and no study linking export orientation to strategic planning was found.

### Cited Findings

**Large Turkish firms**
- Glaister, Dincer, Tatoglu, Demirbag and Zaim 2008, Management Decision 46(3):365-391. Postal survey; sample frame: Istanbul Chamber of Industry (ISO) top 500 manufacturers and Istanbul Stock Exchange firms; 135 usable questionnaires; LISREL. Formal strategic planning has a strong positive relation with firm performance. Environmental turbulence, organization structure and firm size moderate the link [V2] — [Strathprints record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/id/eprint/46148)
- Glaister, Dincer, Tatoglu and Demirbag 2009, Journal of Management Development 28(4):361-379. UK: 113 usable responses (frame 500, EXTEL listed firms); Turkey: 135 (frame 638, ISO 500 and ISE). Contrary to expectations, Turkish firms were more favourably disposed toward strategic planning; UK firms used analysis tools more regularly [V2] — [Strathclyde Pure record](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/a-comparison-of-strategic-planning-practices-in-companies-from-th/)

**Family SMEs: prevalence and who plans**
- Karabulut 2008, Marmara Üniversitesi İİBF Dergisi 25(2), pp. 643 onward. 96 family SMEs producing in Istanbul's textile sector, ISO members, random sampling, e-mail survey, 48% response. Size mix (n=95): 1-9 employees 8.4%, 10-49: 43.2%, 50-99: 30.5%, 100-249: 17.9% (Table 1). 84% had no written strategic plan (79 of 94, Table 8). The general manager was responsible for strategic planning in 80.7%; a planning department in 8.4% (Table 9). 85% planned over 1 to 3 years (Table 10) [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3634)
- Murat and Aydemir 2013, Kocaeli Üniversitesi Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi 26:42-60. Family SMEs registered with KOSGEB in Zonguldak, Bartın and Karabük. Managers' perceptions of strategic planning sub-dimensions did not differ between founders or their relatives and managers with no kinship tie. Scale Cronbach alpha 0.951. Sample size not extracted [V1, partial read] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/251858)

**Size, competition and professional management**
- Zor 2018, Muhasebe ve Finansman Dergisi (April 2018), pp. 183 onward. Telephone survey: 591 SMEs contacted, 200 agreed, of which only 136 said they carry out planning and control activities. Competitive intensity, firm size and management by a professional manager were each positively related to planning and control use; together they explained 20% of its variance. Planning and control explained 26% of performance variance. Note: this measures planning and budgeting, not strategic planning as such [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/453137)

**Generation and institutionalisation (kurumsallaşma)**
- Peksaygılı and Tutan 2015, Journal of Yasar University 10(37). Survey of 44 active first- and second-generation members of the Aegean Automotive Association (EGOD), 60 questions. 60% first generation, 40% second; almost all second-generation respondents still work alongside the first. 82% employ 6-49 people, 11% employ 50-249. Most say mission, vision and objectives are written; fewer report existing strategic plans (exact share not extracted); only 39% have written rules for top management including a family constitution [V1] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/179567)
- Same paper, citing Ankara Sanayi Odası (2005) and Erdoğmuş (2004): basic institutionalisation steps (mission and vision, preparing a strategic plan, restructuring, rules for pay and promotion, job descriptions for family members) usually arise late in the first generation's tenure or while both generations work together, and are more on the second generation's agenda, as a way to manage the handover [V1 for the paper's statement; V3 for the underlying ASO and Erdoğmuş studies] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/179567)
- Kartop and Soyer 2024, Akademik Hassasiyetler 11(26):147-172. Qualitative interviews with Turkish family firms on second-generation handover, leadership, intergenerational conflict and institutionalisation [V1, abstract] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3865884)
- TKYD and TAİDER 2024 next-generation report: an earlier member survey reportedly found one in three families had problems tied to inadequate planning in the handover [V3, search summary] — [TKYD-TAİDER report](https://www.tkyd.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/TKYD-TAIDER-Gelecek-Neslin-Ise-Bakisi-Arastirma-Raporu.pdf)
- Kumaş thesis (Anadolu University): survey of 48 Eskişehir firms on family constitutions and future planning; awareness and perceived importance generally high [V3] — [Anadolu repository](https://acikerisim.anadolu.edu.tr/bitstreams/70b60353-1431-46b7-aa5a-5477d28f96dd/download)
- Yılmaz 2016 master's thesis (Uludağ University): strategic planning and organizational performance in Bursa automotive family firms [V3] — [Uludağ repository](https://acikerisim.uludag.edu.tr/items/15753763-06fa-4790-b5d7-6223c331d206)

**Stage models applied to Turkish firms**
- Bağatır and Kasımoğlu 2024, İslam Ekonomisi ve Finansı Dergisi 10(2):365-393. Survey of 421 partners and senior or middle managers of SMEs at least five years old (first quarter of 2024), on "strategic growth stages" and risk and profitability. The abstract does not name Churchill-Lewis, Greiner or Adizes [V1, abstract] — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3990547)
- YÖK thesis on the relation between life cycle and institutional dimensions in SMEs (reported as a qualitative study of 20 SMEs) [V3] — [YÖK Açık Bilim 69198](https://acikbilim.yok.gov.tr/handle/20.500.12812/69198)
- YÖK thesis on structural change models in growth with an application (search summary says a Kordsa case, 1973-1998) [V3] — [YÖK Açık Bilim 27654](https://acikbilim.yok.gov.tr/handle/20.500.12812/27654)
- Adizes, in a Capital magazine interview (about 2003), said Turkish firms tend not to move beyond the entrepreneurial stage [V3, opinion, dated] — [Capital](https://www.capital.com.tr/yonetim/liderlik/sirketler-nasil-daha-uzun-yasar)
- International contrast: an empirical test of Greiner in family businesses (Brazil) found cumulative, overlapping crises rather than a strict sequence [V3] — [SciELO, BBR](https://www.scielo.br/j/bbr/a/sZVZMhPtNCfSXrhK6sdfndC/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- Karabulut (2008) and Peksaygılı and Tutan (2015) describe SMEs (mostly under 250 staff) where planning is centralised in the general manager and short-horizon. This matches Churchill-Lewis III-G (owner-led planning) more than Stage IV. This mapping is my inference, not tested in the studies.
- The Glaister studies cover large firms only; they do not show what happens in 50-249 employee firms.
- The second-generation entry point appears in Turkish sources as the time when written mission, vision and plans come onto the agenda. This is consistent with Gersick et al.'s ownership transitions, but no Turkish study tested that link directly.

### Gaps
- No Turkish study was found that applies Churchill-Lewis stages to Turkish firms and measures planning per stage.
- No Turkish study was found that relates strategic planning to export orientation or to sector in a tested way.
- No Turkish study was found that reports planning prevalence by firm age or by generation with statistics.
- YÖK thesis full texts could not be downloaded; their sample sizes and findings are unverified.
- Dincer, Tatoglu and Glaister 2006 (Management Research News, Turkish firms' planning process) was not checked.
- PwC Turkey strategic-plan figures for 2018-2019 were not found.

## 3. Validated instruments for measuring strategic planning maturity or need in SMEs

### Takeaway
There are published academic measures of planning formality or sophistication (Boyd and Reuning-Elliott 1998; Bracker and Pearson 1986; Rue and Ibrahim 1998), and one Turkish institutionalisation scale with a strategic-planning sub-scale (Aylan and Koç 2017). None is a licensed commercial "planning need" questionnaire. No licence terms were found for any of them, so item wording should be cleared with the publisher or authors before commercial use.

### Cited Findings
- Boyd and Reuning-Elliott 1998, "A measurement model of strategic planning", Strategic Management Journal 19(2):181-192. Develops and validates a multiple-indicator measure of strategic planning on two independent samples; criticises earlier measures for reliability, validity, dimensionality and crude measurement [V2] — [NAU Experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/a-measurement-model-of-strategic-planning/). Item list not retrieved.
- Bracker and Pearson 1986, Strategic Management Journal 7(6):503-522. A classification of planning-process sophistication in small firms and its link to financial performance [V2] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v7y1986i6p503-522.html). Category labels not verified in this session.
- Rue and Ibrahim 1998, Journal of Small Business Management 36(4):24-32. Survey of 253 small firms. ### Inferences
- For a pre-assessment, the closest published bases are: Rue and Ibrahim's sophistication factors (planning form), Aylan and Koç's 7 strategic-planning items (Turkish wording, though validated on hotel staff), and Gluck's phases as a descriptive ladder. Combining them would be the product's own construction and would need its own validation.
- Using published item wording in a paid product is a licensing question; academic publication does not by itself grant commercial reuse.

### Gaps
- Boyd and Reuning-Elliott item wording and Bracker and Pearson categories were not read.

- No validated "planning need" (as opposed to planning practice) instrument was found, international or Turkish.

## 4. Observable signals in Turkish firms, only where sources support them

### Takeaway
The verified authorities give event and structure signals that a sales team can observe: budget-only planning, capital-planning strain, doubling in size, attained objectives, unexpected results, crisis of control after delegation, second-generation entry, and regulatory or new-market uncertainty. Turkish sources add that planning is usually held by the general manager on a 1 to 3 year horizon, and that larger size, stronger competition and a professional manager go with more planning.

### Cited Findings
- Signal "plans only with the annual budget": Gluck phase one; common in "well over half" of studied firms [V1] — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically)
- Signal "finance struggles to estimate capital needs and financing on a one-year budget": Gluck's trigger to move beyond phase one [V1] — same source
- Signal "doubled or tripled in size in a short time", "original objectives reached", "unexpected success or failure, own or a competitor's": Drucker's warning signs [V1] — [HBR 1994](https://hbr.org/1994/09/the-theory-of-the-business)
- Signal "top management tries to regain control after delegating": Greiner's crisis of control, answered by formal planning procedures [V1] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1998/05/evolution-and-revolution-as-organizations-grow)
- Signal "owner chooses growth over keeping the status quo": Churchill-Lewis III-G, where strategic planning is extensive and owner-led; III-D firms plan with operational budgets [V1] — [HBR 1983](https://hbr.org/1983/05/the-five-stages-of-small-business-growth)
- Signal "facing major regulatory change" (level 2) or "entering an emerging industry or new geographic market" (level 3) [V1] — [Courtney et al.](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf)
- Signal "large, capital-intensive, mature, stable operations": fit for formal strategic programming [V1] — [Mintzberg 1994](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf)
- Turkish signal "second generation working alongside the first": mission, vision and strategic plan come onto the agenda [V1] — [Peksaygılı and Tutan 2015](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/179567)
- Turkish signal "general manager holds planning, no planning unit, 1-3 year horizon" (typical of family SMEs in Istanbul textiles) [V1] — [Karabulut 2008](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/3634)
- Turkish signal "larger size, stronger competition, professional (non-family) manager": more planning and control use [V1] — [Zor 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/453137)
- Turkish signal "ISO 500 or listed manufacturer": positive planning-performance link, moderated by turbulence, structure and size [V2] — [Glaister et al. 2008](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/id/eprint/46148)

### Inferences
- A firm with budget-only planning but executives who know competitors and costs may, per Gluck et al., gain little from formal strategic planning now. That is a disqualifying or "not yet" signal supported by an authority.
- Firms in slow-growing industries may meet only two or three Greiner phases over many years. Sector growth rate is therefore a timing signal, per Greiner.

### Gaps
- No source ties export orientation, sector or firm age to planning need in Turkish firms with tested data.
- None of the signals has been validated as a predictor of buying a planning product.
