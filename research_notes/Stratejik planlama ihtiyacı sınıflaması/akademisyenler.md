## 1. Peter Drucker: what planning is and is not, who needs it, when to revisit the theory of the business

### Takeaway
Drucker does not classify firms by planning need. He defines long-range planning as entrepreneurial decision-making about the future impact of present decisions, says it is not forecasting, and gives triggers for revisiting the "theory of the business" (objectives reached, rapid growth, unexpected success or failure). These triggers are timing criteria, not a firm typology.

### Cited Findings
- Drucker, "Long-Range Planning: Challenge to Management Science", *Management Science* 5(3):238-249, 1959. A database abstract describes it as defining long-range planning as "the organized process of making entrepreneurial decisions" [V2] — [EconBiz record](https://econbiz.de/Record/long-range-planning-challenge-to-management-science-drucker-peter/10009190865); [INFORMS DOI](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.5.3.238); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:5:y:1959:i:3:p:238-249)
- A reprint chapter of the same Drucker text opens by saying the concept is easier to define by what it is not. The first misconception it names is that planning is forecasting [V3, seen via search snippet of the chapter] — [O'Reilly reprint chapter](https://oreilly.com/library/view/peter-f-drucker/9781647820275/10394/OEBPS/xhtml/chapter008.xhtml)
- Drucker 1959 (prior knowledge): long-range planning is also not "making future decisions" and not an attempt to eliminate risk. It deals with the futurity of present decisions. Its aim is to take the right risks [V4] — [INFORMS DOI](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.5.3.238)
- Drucker 1957 speaking notes on long-range planning cover what it is and is not, why it is needed, and who uses it [V3, archive record] — [Calisphere archive record](https://calisphere.org/item/d75bf714babb27995bbbfb88d02f8992)
- Drucker, "The Theory of the Business", *HBR*, September-October 1994. A theory of the business has three sets of assumptions: about the environment (society, market, customer, technology), about the specific mission, and about the core competencies needed [V3] — [Managing Research Library summary](https://managingresearchlibrary.org/node/852); [Drucker Institute archivist note](https://drucker.institute/?p=13742)
- Drucker 1994: a valid theory must fit reality, the three parts must fit each other, it must be known throughout the organization, and it must be tested constantly [V3] — [Managing Research Library summary](https://managingresearchlibrary.org/node/852)
- Drucker 1994: when an organization reaches its original objectives, its theory may become obsolete, so it must review environment, mission and competencies [V3] — [Managing Research Library summary](https://managingresearchlibrary.org/node/852)
- Drucker 1994 (prior knowledge): further warning signs are rapid growth, unexpected success, and unexpected failure (own or a competitor's). He recommends "preventive care": systematic abandonment, asking every three years whether the firm would enter each product, policy or channel today. The "every three years" interval could NOT be confirmed in this session [V4] — [HBR 1994 bibliographic link (paywalled)](https://hbr.org/1994/09/the-theory-of-the-business)
- Drucker, *Managing in Turbulent Times* (1980) and *Innovation and Entrepreneurship* (1985): content not retrieved in this session. Prior knowledge: the 1985 book lists seven sources of innovative opportunity (starting with the unexpected) and treats entrepreneurship as a systematic, manageable discipline, not a planning document [V4] — no source URL found in session

### Inferences
- For the SaaS, Drucker supports "event triggers" (objective reached, fast growth, surprise result) as the moment to plan, not a size or sector class.
- Drucker's definition (decisions about futurity) means a firm with long-lived commitments (capital, brands, people) has more to plan. This is an inference, not Drucker's stated classification.

### Gaps
- Full text of the 1959 article and the 1994 article was not accessible. The "every three years" abandonment detail is unverified.
- No content from *Managing in Turbulent Times* (1980) was retrieved.

## 2. Henry Mintzberg: when formal planning works, its fallacies, deliberate vs emergent, Strategy Safari

### Takeaway
Mintzberg is the clearest authority on "when formal planning fits": it works as strategic programming of already-chosen strategies in stable, predictable, large, capital-intensive, tightly coupled organizations (machine bureaucracies). Outside these conditions it can harm learning. He says planning cannot create strategy because prediction, detachment and formalization are fallacies.

### Cited Findings
- Mintzberg, "The Fall and Rise of Strategic Planning", *HBR*, January-February 1994. Strategic planning arrived in the mid-1960s and was adopted as "the one best way" to build strategy. It followed Taylor by separating thinking from doing. The author says this has "not exactly worked out that way" [V1, introduction only] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1994/01/the-fall-and-rise-of-strategic-planning)
- Mintzberg, *The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning*, Free Press, 1994. The table of contents lists the fallacies of predetermination, detachment and formalization. Some listings say "prediction" or "formulation", so wording should be checked in the book [V3] — [WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/28675146); [Buresund book notes](https://buresund.nu/books/the-rise-and-fall-of-strategic-planning)
- Mintzberg 1994: the "grand fallacy" is the belief that analysis can produce synthesis, so planning equals strategy making [V3, blog reposting text] — [Thinkers50 summary](https://thinkers50.com/blog/mintzberg-on-strategic-planning/)
- Mintzberg, "Rethinking Strategic Planning" Part II (on mintzberg.org): what is called strategic planning is really strategic programming. Its role is to put settled, viable strategies into effect, not to devise them [V3, search summary of Mintzberg's own PDF; the PDF is a scanned image and could not be read directly] — [mintzberg.org Part II PDF](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf)
- Same source: programming fits contexts that are fairly predictable or controlled, often mature industries. The organization tends to be large and capital intensive, with simple, tightly coupled operations. Examples are machine bureaucracies in airlines, retail banking, much mass production and the post office. Outside these conditions programming can crowd out the flexibility needed to learn [V3] — [mintzberg.org Part II PDF](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf); [Part I PDF](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_1.pdf)
- Mintzberg, "Structure in 5's" (1980): the machine bureaucracy is found in simple, stable environments, often in older, larger organizations [V3] — [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/doc/293225804/Mintzberg-Structure)
- Mintzberg 1994 HBR (prior knowledge): planners can still play useful roles as finders of strategy, analysts and catalysts. Plans serve communication and control [V4] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1994/01/the-fall-and-rise-of-strategic-planning)
- Mintzberg and Waters, "Of Strategies, Deliberate and Emergent", *Strategic Management Journal* 6(3):257-272, 1985. Deliberate and emergent strategies are two ends of a continuum. Eight types lie between: planned, entrepreneurial, ideological, umbrella, process, unconnected, consensus and imposed [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:6:y:1985:i:3:p:257-272)
- Mintzberg and Waters 1985: planned strategies appear in organizations that extrapolate established patterns in environments expected to stay stable [V3] — [Scribd copy of paper](https://www.scribd.com/document/499607523/mintzberg-of-strategies)
- Mintzberg and Waters 1985 (prior knowledge): an "imposed" strategy comes from outside the organization (for example a parent company or government), which pre-empts internal choice. An "entrepreneurial" strategy lives in the vision of one leader [V4] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:6:y:1985:i:3:p:257-272)
- Mintzberg, Ahlstrand and Lampel, *Strategy Safari* (1998), ten schools: design, planning, positioning, entrepreneurial, cognitive, learning, power, cultural, environmental, configuration [V3] — [CIO Wiki summary](https://cio-wiki.com/wiki/Mintzberg%27s_10_Schools_of_Strategic_Thought)
- *Strategy Safari* 1998 (prior knowledge): the configuration school says different schools fit different stages and contexts. Examples: the entrepreneurial school fits start-up and turnaround, the planning school fits machine organizations in stable conditions, the learning school fits complex, unpredictable settings and professional or adhocracy forms [V4] — [CIO Wiki summary](https://cio-wiki.com/wiki/Mintzberg%27s_10_Schools_of_Strategic_Thought)

### Inferences
- Many 50-499 employee Turkish family firms will not match Mintzberg's machine-bureaucracy profile. Mintzberg would point them to planning as programming and communication of a strategy already chosen by the leader, not as strategy creation.
- Mintzberg's conditions are about the fit of **formal** planning. They are not a claim that such firms need no strategy.

### Gaps
- Mintzberg's own PDFs are scanned. The exact wording of the conditions was seen only through a search summary.
- The configuration-to-school mapping in *Strategy Safari* is from prior knowledge, not verified in session.

## 3. Igor Ansoff: turbulence levels and matching strategic aggressiveness and capability

### Takeaway
Ansoff gives the most explicit "environment class to planning type" mapping: five turbulence levels, each needing a matching strategic aggressiveness and management capability. Extrapolative planning fits low levels. At levels 4 and 5 extrapolation is dangerous.

### Cited Findings
- Ansoff's model has five turbulence levels, from placid and predictable to highly changeable and unpredictable [V3] — [secondary summary, Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/doc/78926503/Managing-in-Troubulent-Environment); [Syscoi summary](https://stream.syscoi.com/2019/04/15/h-igor-ansoff-strategic-posture)
- Ansoff's strategic success formula: for optimum return, both strategic aggressiveness and capability must match environmental turbulence [V3] — [ANZAM 2013 paper](https://www.anzam.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf-manager/16_ANZAM-2013-016.PDF)
- At levels 4 and 5, profits do not follow growth, extrapolation of the past is dangerous, and surprises are frequent [V3] — [ANZAM 2013 paper](https://www.anzam.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf-manager/16_ANZAM-2013-016.PDF)
- Ansoff built diagnostic instruments to assess a firm's turbulence level and its alignment [V3] — [ANZAM 2013 paper](https://www.anzam.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf-manager/16_ANZAM-2013-016.PDF)
- A study of Brazilian hospitality firms found support for the paradigm, with aggressiveness and responsiveness mediating turbulence and performance [V3] — [secondary search summary; Uece dissertation](https://www.uece.br/ppga/wp-content/uploads/sites/49/2020/08/Dissertação-Vitor.pdf)
- Ansoff and McDonnell, *Implanting Strategic Management* (2nd ed., 1990), prior knowledge: level names are roughly repetitive, expanding, changing, discontinuous, surprising. Matching aggressiveness: stable, reactive, anticipatory, entrepreneurial, creative. Matching management mode moves from control by procedures, to long-range planning (extrapolation), to strategic planning, to strategic issue management and weak-signal management at the highest levels [V4] — [RePEc chapter record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:pal:palchp:978-0-230-59060-1_6)

### Inferences
- Ansoff's scale could be used as a published basis for a turbulence self-assessment question in onboarding. The exact level wording must be checked in the 1990 book first.

### Gaps
- The 1990 book text was not accessed. The level-by-level names and management systems are V4.

## 4. Life-cycle and growth-stage models: Greiner, Churchill and Lewis, Adizes, Scott and Bruce

### Takeaway
Stage models do link formal planning to stage. Churchill and Lewis (1983) say formal planning is minimal at "Existence", is mainly cash forecasting at "Survival", and becomes operational then strategic planning in later stages. Greiner (1972) links planning and control systems to the "coordination" phase after a crisis of control.

### Cited Findings
- Churchill and Lewis, "The Five Stages of Small Business Growth", *HBR*, May-June 1983. Stages: existence, survival, success, take-off, resource maturity [V2/V3] — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1504517); [HBR](https://hbr.org/1983/05/the-five-stages-of-small-business-growth)
- Churchill and Lewis 1983: systems and formal planning are minimal to nonexistent in Existence. In Survival, formal planning is at best cash forecasting. In Success, systems are installed ahead of need, with budget-driven planning. Later stages bring sound systems, procedures and controls [V3] — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/founder-led-companies/churchill-lewis-small-business-growth); [growthforce PDF link, 404 in session](https://www.growthforce.com/hubfs/The_Five_Stages_of_Small_Business_Growth.pdf)
- Churchill and Lewis 1983 (prior knowledge): Success has two sub-stages, Disengagement and Growth. Take-off features operational and strategic planning. Resource Maturity features extensive formal planning. The owner's "strategic abilities" grow in importance as the firm moves up stages, while the owner's own doing abilities decline in importance. The owner's goals are one of eight management factors [V4] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1983/05/the-five-stages-of-small-business-growth)
- Critics say the model suits teaching but real firms are messier [V3] — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/founder-led-companies/churchill-lewis-small-business-growth)
- Greiner, "Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow", *HBR*, July-August 1972. Five phases: creativity, direction, delegation, coordination, collaboration. Crises: leadership, autonomy, control, red tape, and an unnamed fifth. A practice that works in one phase can cause the next crisis [V3] — [Wikipedia, Larry E. Greiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_E._Greiner); [UC Merced copy of article](https://naturalsciences.ucmerced.edu/sites/g/files/ufvvjh1296/f/page/documents/evolution_and_revolution_as_organizations_grow.pdf)
- Greiner 1998 HBR reprint added a sixth phase (alliances, crisis of identity) per Wikipedia. Sources conflict on this [V3] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_E._Greiner)
- Greiner 1972 (prior knowledge): the coordination phase brings formal planning procedures and product groups. These solve the crisis of control but later cause the red-tape crisis [V4] — [UC Merced copy](https://naturalsciences.ucmerced.edu/sites/g/files/ufvvjh1296/f/page/documents/evolution_and_revolution_as_organizations_grow.pdf)
- Scott and Bruce, "Five Stages of Growth in Small Business", *Long Range Planning* 20(3), 1987 (prior knowledge): stages inception, survival, growth, expansion, maturity. Planning moves from informal to formal and long-range at later stages [V4] — no URL retrieved
- Adizes, *Corporate Lifecycles* (1988) (prior knowledge): stages from Courtship and Infancy through Go-Go, Adolescence, Prime, to aging stages. "Prime" balances flexibility and control [V4] — no URL retrieved

### Inferences
- A firm with 50-499 employees is usually past Churchill-Lewis "Survival". By their model it is at the stage where systems and, later, strategic planning become needed. This is an inference from the model, not tested on Turkish firms.
- Greiner suggests planning systems are a response to a specific crisis (loss of control after delegation). That is a timing signal for sales.

### Gaps
- Primary texts of Churchill-Lewis, Scott-Bruce and Adizes were not accessed. Stage-by-stage planning detail beyond Survival is V4.

## 5. Complexity frameworks: Cynefin and the Stacey agreement and certainty matrix

### Takeaway
Cynefin and Stacey classify **situations**, not firms. Analysis and expert plans fit ordered domains (simple or complicated). Complex situations need safe-to-fail experiments. Chaotic situations need immediate action. In Stacey's matrix, technical-rational planning fits only the corner close to certainty and agreement.

### Cited Findings
- Snowden and Boone, "A Leader's Framework for Decision Making", *HBR*, November 2007, introduced Cynefin for leaders with four domains: simple, complicated, complex, chaotic (plus disorder) [V3] — [Cynefin Co page](https://thecynefin.co/a-leaders-framework-for-decision-making/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- Simple: sense, categorize, respond (best practice). Complex: probe, sense, respond, with experiments that can safely fail. Chaotic: the leader must first "staunch the bleeding" [V3] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- Snowden and Boone 2007 (prior knowledge): complicated domain is sense, analyze, respond (good practice, experts). Chaotic is act, sense, respond [V4] — [Cynefin Co page](https://thecynefin.co/a-leaders-framework-for-decision-making/)
- Domain names changed later: "simple" became "obvious" (2014) and now "clear" [V3] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- Stacey's agreement and certainty matrix: most management theory addresses the region close to certainty and agreement, where technical-rational decisions apply [V3] — [Complexity theory handout](https://www.essentialgptrainingbook.com/wp-content/online-resources/ch10/complexity%20theory%20-%20the%20stacey%20diagram.doc)
- Zimmerman's version of the matrix names a central "zone of complexity" where traditional management is not very effective [V3] — [NCCMT PDF](https://www.nccmt.ca/uploads/media/media/0001/03/219b71c0f7ff72221e90bd4b6b7c537466c18301.pdf); [Plexus Institute](https://plexusinstitute.org/edgeware/archive/think/main_aides3.html)
- Reported account: Stacey later moved away from the matrix and did not want it to carry his name [V3, informal transcript] — [University of Waterloo handout](https://uwaterloo.ca/waterloo-institute-for-social-innovation-and-resilience/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/9._complex_problems-the_agreement_certainty_matrix-accessibility2.pdf)
- Popular summaries map Stacey zones to simple, complicated, complex, chaotic. This mapping conflicts with Zimmerman's original zones [V3] — [SFU page](https://sfu.ca/complex-systems-frameworks/frameworks/complex-vs-complicated/stacey-matrix.html)

### Inferences
- Cynefin supports "crisis first, plan later" (chaotic domain). It is a situation test that can change over time for the same firm.
- Stacey's own disowning of the matrix weakens it as an "authority" citation.

### Gaps
- The full HBR 2007 text was not read in session.

## 6. Small business and new venture planning research

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses find a positive but context-dependent planning-performance link in small firms. Newness of the firm weakens or moderates the link. Brinckmann et al. (2010) recommend combining planning and learning. Timing matters (Greene and Hopp 2017). Lean startup authors argue very early ventures should search and test, not execute a plan.

### Cited Findings
- Robinson and Pearce, "Research Thrusts in Small Firm Strategic Planning", *Academy of Management Review* 9(1):128-137, 1984. Reviewed over 50 small-firm planning studies and identified four research thrusts [V2] — [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/07a34aa2a24e4ff83cebe0e040561e5e)
- Robinson and Pearce 1984 (prior knowledge): reasons small firms do not plan include lack of time, lack of knowledge of planning, lack of expertise, and lack of trust or openness [V4] — [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/07a34aa2a24e4ff83cebe0e040561e5e)
- Schwenk and Shrader, "Effects of Formal Strategic Planning on Financial Performance in Small Firms: A Meta-Analysis", *Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice* 17(3):53-64, 1993. Found formal strategic planning beneficial for small firms (significant positive association across studies). Study counts differ between secondary accounts (14 vs 26) [V2/V3] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/entthe/v17y1993i3p53-64.html)
- Brinckmann, Grichnik and Kapsa, "Should entrepreneurs plan or just storm the castle?", *Journal of Business Venturing* 25(1):24-40, 2010. Planning is beneficial, but newness of the firm and cultural environment significantly moderate the link. They propose a "concomitant and dynamic approach" combining planning and learning [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbvent:v:25:y:2010:i:1:p:24-40); [Effectuation library](https://effectuation.org/publications-library/should-entrepreneurs-plan-or-just-storm-the-castle-a-meta-analysis-on-contextual-factors-impacting-the-business-planning-performance-relationship-in-small-firms)
- Brinckmann et al. 2010 (prior knowledge): the planning-performance link was stronger for established small firms than for new firms. Cultural uncertainty avoidance also moderated it [V4] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbvent:v:25:y:2010:i:1:p:24-40)
- Delmar and Shane, "Does business planning facilitate the development of new ventures?", *Strategic Management Journal* 24(12):1165-1185, 2003. Using 223 Swedish new ventures from 1998, planning reduced the chance of disbanding and sped up product development and organizing. They call planning an "important precursor to action" [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:24:y:2003:i:12:p:1165-1185); [Lund University record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/f3d170c5-75f5-4620-ae5f-92c3f1161e0a)
- Greene and Hopp, "Are Formal Planners More Likely to Achieve New Venture Viability? A Counterfactual Model and Analysis", *Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal* 11:36-60, 2017. Note: the title in the brief ("When should entrepreneurs write their business plans") is from press coverage, not the paper [V2] — [BFH repository](https://arbor.bfh.ch/11991)
- Greene and Hopp 2017 (press coverage): formal planners were about 16% more likely to reach viability. Best results came from plans written roughly 6-12 months after deciding to start, alongside customer and product work. Coverage disagrees on optimal duration [V3] — [FE News](https://fenews.co.uk/?p=14536); [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/startupsmart/when-is-the-ideal-time-for-entrepreneurs-to-write-a-business-plan/)
- Hopp and Greene, "In Pursuit of Time", *Journal of Management Studies* 55:320-351, 2018, covers sequencing and timing of start-up activities [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jomstd:v:55:y:2018:i:2:p:320-351)
- Blank, "Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything", *HBR* 91(5):63-72, May 2013. Argues lean methods should spread from tech start-ups to small businesses and corporate initiatives [V2/V3] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/05/why-the-lean-start-up-changes-everything); [Blank's blog](https://steveblank.com/2013/04/16/when-hell-froze-over-in-the-harvard-business-review/)
- Blank 2013 (prior knowledge): a start-up searches for a business model, while an established firm executes one. Business plans rarely survive first contact with customers, so founders should test hypotheses first. The "first contact" wording was NOT confirmed in session [V4] — [Gustavus PDF copy](https://gustavus.edu/academics/departments/business-and-economics/concertFiles/media/2020_Gustie_Cup/Blank_2013.pdf)
- Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011) (prior knowledge): build-measure-learn and validated learning under extreme uncertainty [V4] — no URL retrieved

### Inferences
- The evidence is not "small firms should not plan". It is "planning helps, less so for very new firms, and best when combined with learning". Delmar and Shane (2003) conflict with the lean-startup position for early ventures.
- For founders, the research supports a learning-first mode before formal planning. For established 50-499 firms, the meta-analyses support formal planning.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes of the meta-analyses were not retrieved. Greene and Hopp timing percentages are from press coverage only.

## 7. Family business: Carlock and Ward, three-circle model, Gersick et al.

### Takeaway
Family-business scholars say family firms need two parallel plans (family and business) because family, ownership and business overlap. The need grows with ownership complexity (controlling owner, sibling partnership, cousin consortium). Succession is a key trigger, but Carlock and Ward say succession and estate planning alone are too narrow. The process requires family commitment, which is a readiness condition.

### Cited Findings
- Carlock and Ward, *Strategic Planning for the Family Business: Parallel Planning to Unify the Family and Business*, Palgrave, 2001. Equal weight to family and business plans and their integration [V3] — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/archive/strategic-planning-for-the-family-business-parallel-planning-to-unite-the-family-and-business); [getAbstract summary](https://www.getabstract.com/en/summary/strategic-planning-for-the-family-business/2071)
- Carlock and Ward 2001: family business planning traditionally focused on estate planning and succession. The authors see these as too limited [V3, book description] — [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2001/strategic-planning-for-the-family-business-parallel-planning-to?p=1)
- The parallel planning process starts from family values and business philosophy, and requires a commitment to ongoing conversations, documented decisions and measured outcomes [V3, consultant summary] — [FBCG summary](https://www.thefbcg.com/resource/strategic-planning-for-the-family-business-parallel-planning-to-unify-the-family-and-business/)
- Carlock and Ward, *When Family Businesses Are Best: The Parallel Planning Process for Family Harmony and Business Success*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2010 [V2] — [Springer](https://www.springer.com/9780230222625)
- Application study: "How Do We Stop Trying to Do It All? Application of the Family Business Parallel Planning Process" (SAGE) [V2, title only] — [SAGE figshare](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/_How_Do_We_Stop_Trying_to_Do_It_All_Application_of_the_Family_Business_Parallel_Planning_Process/4944015)
- Carlock and Ward 2001 (prior knowledge): the business strategy depends on the family's commitment. Strategic options are matched to family commitment and to business potential [V4] — [Palgrave](https://www.palgrave.com/la/book/9780333947319)
- Gersick, Davis, Hampton and Lansberg, *Generation to Generation: Life Cycles of the Family Business*, HBS Press, 1997 (some sources say 1996). Three developmental axes: ownership, family, business. Ownership stages: Controlling Owner, Sibling Partnership, Cousin Consortium. Built on the three-circle model [V3] — [CFEG summary](https://cfeg.com/about/thought-leadership-stages); [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1996/generation-to-generation-life-cycles-of-the-family-business)
- Gersick et al. 1997: Sibling Partnership tensions include power and fairness, dividends vs reinvestment, and building professional systems [V3] — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/family-business/family-business-lifecycle)
- Tagiuri and Davis three-circle model (family, ownership, business), from their 1982 Harvard working paper, published as "Bivalent Attributes of the Family Firm", *Family Business Review* 9(2), 1996 [V4] — no URL retrieved

### Inferences
- For Turkish family firms of 50-499 employees, the authorities suggest second-generation entry, upcoming succession, or a move to sibling ownership as moments of high planning need.
- Family unwillingness or conflict is treated as a readiness barrier, not as proof that planning is unneeded.

### Gaps
- Primary text of Carlock and Ward was not accessed. Commitment-matching detail is V4.
- No Turkish-specific family business planning study was searched (out of scope).

## 8. Other authorities: Rumelt, Porter, Hamel and Prahalad, Grant, Kaplan and Norton

### Takeaway
Rumelt says strategy starts with a diagnosis of a real challenge. Grant (2003) shows that in a turbulent industry large firms kept planning but changed it into "planned emergence". Porter, Hamel and Prahalad, and Kaplan and Norton were not verified in session.

### Cited Findings
- Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*, 2011. The kernel is diagnosis (explains the challenge), guiding policy, and coherent actions. Without diagnosis there is nothing to judge the policy against [V3] — [Kernel concept note](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/kernel-of-good-strategy/); [CustomerThink summary](https://customerthink.com/good_strategy_bad_strategy_what_is_the_kernel_of_a_strategy_part_ii_diagnosis/)
- Rumelt, *The Crux*, 2022 (prior knowledge): strategy is a response to a challenge, focused on the "crux", the most critical and addressable part of the challenge [V4] — no URL retrieved
- Grant, "Strategic planning in a turbulent environment: Evidence from the oil majors", *Strategic Management Journal* 24(6):491-517, 2003. Eight large oil companies changed planning after the late 1970s. Planning became "planned emergence": coordinating decentralized strategy within performance targets and corporate guidelines. It supported adaptation but showed limited innovation and analysis [V2] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i6p491-517.html)
- Porter, "What Is Strategy?", *HBR*, November-December 1996 (prior knowledge): operational effectiveness is not strategy. Strategy means a unique, valuable position, trade-offs and fit [V4] — no URL retrieved
- Hamel and Prahalad, "Strategic Intent", *HBR*, 1989 (prior knowledge): ambition beyond current resources, not fit-based planning [V4] — no URL retrieved
- Kaplan and Norton, *The Execution Premium*, 2008 (prior knowledge): a closed-loop strategy management system linking strategy to operations [V4] — no URL retrieved

### Inferences
- Rumelt gives a "need" test: is there a real challenge to diagnose? Grant shows turbulence changes the type of planning, not whether planning is used, in large firms.

### Gaps
- Porter, Hamel-Prahalad and Kaplan-Norton were not searched. Their content is V4 only.

## 9. "When not to do formal strategic planning": which items are supported, by whom, and are they need or readiness criteria

### Takeaway
No author offers a single "do not plan" list. Several items are supported indirectly. Most of them are **readiness or timing** criteria (not now, or not formal), not criteria that the firm never needs strategy. "Short-lived business" found no support in this session.

### Cited Findings
Distinction used: a **need criterion** says whether the situation calls for formal strategic planning at all. A **readiness or timing criterion** says whether the process can work now.

- **Crisis.** Snowden and Boone 2007: in chaotic situations leaders must first act to stabilize, then move to other modes [V3] — [Wikipedia, Cynefin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework). Classification: timing. Planning is postponed, not rejected.
- **Very early idea or new venture.** Brinckmann et al. 2010: newness moderates the planning benefit, and planning should be combined with learning [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbvent:v:25:y:2010:i:1:p:24-40). Blank 2013: search and test before executing a plan [V2/V3] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/05/why-the-lean-start-up-changes-everything). Churchill and Lewis 1983: minimal formal planning in Existence [V3] — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/founder-led-companies/churchill-lewis-small-business-growth). Conflicting: Delmar and Shane 2003 found early planning helps [V2] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:24:y:2003:i:12:p:1165-1185). Greene and Hopp 2017 found timing matters [V3] — [FE News](https://fenews.co.uk/?p=14536). Classification: timing and type (learning mode first), with conflicting evidence.
- **Unstable, unpredictable environment.** Mintzberg: programming fits predictable, stable contexts and can harm learning elsewhere [V3] — [mintzberg.org Part II](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf). Ansoff: extrapolation is dangerous at turbulence levels 4 and 5 [V3] — [ANZAM 2013](https://www.anzam.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf-manager/16_ANZAM-2013-016.PDF). Grant 2003: large firms adapted planning rather than dropped it [V2] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i6p491-517.html). Classification: need for a **different type**, not for no planning.
- **Unwilling decision maker or uncommitted family.** Carlock and Ward: parallel planning requires commitment to ongoing conversation and documented decisions [V3] — [FBCG summary](https://www.thefbcg.com/resource/strategic-planning-for-the-family-business-parallel-planning-to-unify-the-family-and-business/). Robinson and Pearce 1984 list owner-side barriers (V4) — [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/07a34aa2a24e4ff83cebe0e040561e5e). Classification: readiness.
- **Strategy decided elsewhere.** Mintzberg and Waters 1985 name an "imposed" strategy type [V2 for the type name, V4 for its definition] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:6:y:1985:i:3:p:257-272). Grant 2003: business units plan within corporate guidelines [V2] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i6p491-517.html). Classification: scope. The unit still programs and implements, but does not choose the strategy.
- **No real choice.** Rumelt 2011: strategy needs a diagnosed challenge [V3] — [Kernel note](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/kernel-of-good-strategy/). Porter 1996 (trade-offs) is V4. Classification: need criterion, weakly supported (by inference from definitions, not an explicit "do not plan" rule).
- **Short-lived business.** No author found in this session states this. Drucker's "futurity of present decisions" idea (V4) could imply a short planning horizon, but this is inference only. Classification: unsupported.
- **Stable, large, capital-intensive firm.** Mintzberg treats this as the case where formal planning **does** fit (as programming) [V3] — [mintzberg.org Part II](https://mintzberg.org/sites/default/files/article/download/rethinking_strategic_planning_2.pdf). Classification: need criterion for formal planning.

### Inferences
- The only true "need" classifications found are environmental (Ansoff turbulence, Mintzberg stability, Cynefin domain), developmental (Churchill-Lewis, Greiner, Gersick), and challenge-based (Rumelt). The "when not to plan" items are mostly readiness and timing.
- No established single classification maps firm type to planning type and timing. The literature offers several partial lenses. A product classification would have to combine them, and that combination would be the company's own framework, not an authority's.

### Gaps
- No source states "short-lived business" as a reason not to plan.
- No author was found who lists "crisis, no choice, early idea, unwilling owner, decided elsewhere" together as one set. The list appears to be a synthesis, not a published classification.
- Turkish-context validation of any framework was not searched.
