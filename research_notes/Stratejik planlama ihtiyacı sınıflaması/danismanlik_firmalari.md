# Consulting-firm classifications of companies by need for, and type of, strategic planning

Research date: 16 November 2026 (Monday). Scope: McKinsey, BCG, Bain, PwC Strategy&, Deloitte/Monitor, EY, KPMG.
Source-access legend used below:
- [PRIMARY-READ] = the firm's own page or article text was fetched and read in this session.
- [PRIMARY-UNREAD] = primary source exists and is cited, but fetch failed (timeouts on mckinsey.com) or only a search snippet was seen. Details marked this way come from search snippets and/or the researcher's prior knowledge of the text and should be checked against the original before quoting.
- [SECONDARY] = only aggregator, review or press summaries were seen.

Overall finding up front: none of the major firms publishes a single "who needs strategic planning" segmentation for customer targeting. What exists are (a) planning-evolution / maturity stages (McKinsey 1980), (b) environment-based style or posture choices (McKinsey 1997, BCG 2012/2015), (c) growth-crisis stages (Bain 2016), and (d) process critiques (Bain/Marakon 2006, McKinsey 2018). Several imply that some firms need lighter or different planning, but none states that a class of firm needs no strategic planning at all.

## McKinsey: planning evolution phases, uncertainty levels, power curve, and work on small firms

### Takeaway
McKinsey offers two classic classifications usable for targeting: the Gluck-Kaufman-Walleck four phases (a maturity ladder of a firm's planning system, 1980) and the Courtney-Kirkland-Viguerie four levels of residual uncertainty (an environment classification that tells which planning tools fit, 1997). The 2018 "Strategy Beyond the Hockey Stick" work classifies firms by position on an economic-profit power curve and argues the planning process itself is distorted by a "social side"; no McKinsey SME-specific planning classification was found.

### Cited Findings
**Gluck, Kaufman and Walleck (1980), "Strategic Management for Competitive Advantage", HBR July-August 1980, vol. 58 no. 4**
- The article lays out four sequential phases of planning-system evolution: (1) basic financial planning, (2) forecast-based planning, (3) externally oriented planning, (4) strategic management — [McKinsey Quarterly, "Thinking strategically" (republication/retrospective)](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically) [PRIMARY-UNREAD: search snippet only, page fetch timed out]; [HBR listing via ProQuest](https://www.proquest.com/docview/227788805) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]; [Wikipedia "Externally oriented planning"](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Externally_oriented_planning) [SECONDARY]
- Method: the authors studied planning systems of large corporations and judged the quality of the plans produced, not just the process — [McKinsey Quarterly, "Thinking strategically"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- McKinsey's retrospective says a company cannot skip a phase, because each phase builds attitudes and capabilities the next one needs — [McKinsey Quarterly, "Thinking strategically"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Only phase 4 merges strategic planning with everyday management, and the authors reported that very few companies had reached it — same source [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Gluck's team had earlier produced a 1978 McKinsey staff paper, "The evolution of strategic management" — same source [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Phase "value systems" commonly attributed to the article (researcher's prior knowledge, NOT verified in this session): phase 1 "meet budget", phase 2 "predict the future", phase 3 "think strategically", phase 4 "create the future". Phase 1 centres on annual budgets and functional focus; phase 2 on multi-year forecasts; phase 3 on market and competitor analysis and strategic alternatives; phase 4 on a planning framework, process and values woven into management — [SlideShare copy of the 1980 HBR article](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/strategic-management-for-competitive-advantage/47079934) [PRIMARY-UNREAD; verify]

**Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie (1997), "Strategy Under Uncertainty", HBR November-December 1997 (McKinsey authors; republished by McKinsey)**
- Core claim: traditional planning that relies on precise single-point predictions tends to understate uncertainty; the authors offer a four-level framework of "residual uncertainty" — [McKinsey PDF of the article](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD: fetch timed out; snippet seen]; [Brandeis copy](https://heller.brandeis.edu/executive-education/pdfs/Strategy-Under-Certain.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- Level 1 "a clear enough future": managers can develop a single forecast that is precise enough to base strategy on. Level 2 "alternate futures": analysis narrows outcomes to a few discrete scenarios but cannot say which will occur (example: major regulatory change). Level 4 "true ambiguity": multiple interacting uncertainties make the environment virtually impossible to predict — [McKinsey PDF](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) [snippet]; [Aarhus University paper, "Strategy Under Uncertainty 25 yrs on"](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/448378073/Strategy_Under_Uncertainty_25yrs_On_R3_copy.pdf) [SECONDARY, snippet]
- Level 3 "a range of futures" (a continuous range of outcomes with no natural scenarios), the three postures — shape the future, adapt to the future, reserve the right to play — and the three move types — big bets, options, no-regrets moves — are from the researcher's knowledge of the article; search snippets did not confirm the exact labels in this session — [McKinsey PDF](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD; verify before quoting]
- The Mondex example illustrates shaping: the firm tried to set universal electronic-cash standards — [McKinsey PDF](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) [snippet]
- Courtney later wrote on shaper versus adapter postures under Knightian uncertainty ("To shape or adapt") — [Courtney et al. paper PDF](https://d30i16bbj53pdg.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/To-shape-or-adapt-knowledge-problems-epistemologies-and-strategic-postures-under-knightian-uncertainty.pdf) [SECONDARY, snippet only]

**Bradley, Hirt and Smit (2018), "Strategy Beyond the Hockey Stick" (Wiley), and McKinsey Quarterly "Strategy to beat the odds" (2018)**
- Firms plotted by economic profit form a power curve, not a bell curve: long flat middle, steep tails — [McKinsey Quarterly, "Strategy to beat the odds"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategy-to-beat-the-odds) [PRIMARY-UNREAD: fetch timed out, snippet]; [McKinsey PDF](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Business%20Functions/Strategy%20and%20Corporate%20Finance/Our%20Insights/Strategy%20to%20beat%20the%20odds/Strategy-to-beat-the-odds.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- A company in the middle has about an 8% chance of moving to the top quintile; one or two big moves raise the odds to about 17%, three big moves to about 47% — [Consulting Magazine Q&A with the authors, 2018](https://www.consultingmag.com/amp/2018/04/12/qa-big-moves-make-the-difference-mckinsey-company-authors-reveal-what-really-matters-for-exceptional-performance/) [SECONDARY]; [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategy-to-beat-the-odds) [snippet]
- The "social side of strategy" (corporate politics, incentives, biases) explains why plans become "hockey sticks" and why resources get spread thinly ("peanut butter" allocation) — [Antoine Buteau book summary](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/strategy-beyond-the-hockey-stick-book-summary.md) [SECONDARY]; [CFO.com](https://cfo.com/news/a-fresh-approach-to-business-strategies/659330/) [SECONDARY]
- A secondary summary attributes roughly 30% / 25% / 45% of the odds to endowment, trends and moves respectively; this split was seen only in one secondary source — [Substack summary](https://productandrew.substack.com/p/product-92) [SECONDARY; verify]
- The research base is the largest listed companies (non-financial global large caps); it was not designed for SMEs — researcher's knowledge; not verified in this session [Gap-adjacent]

### Inferences
- Gluck et al. is the closest McKinsey analogue to a "planning maturity" classification. A vendor could map prospects to phase 1-4 by observable behaviour (budget only, multi-year forecast, competitor/market analysis, integrated strategic management) and position the product as the step to the next phase. Because the model says phases cannot be skipped, a phase-1 firm would be sold forecast-based tooling, not full strategic management.
- Courtney et al. classify the environment, not the firm. Level 1 firms can live with conventional forecast-based planning; levels 2-4 need scenario, options and posture choices. A vendor could segment by sector volatility (e.g., regulated or tech-disrupted sectors at levels 2-4).
- The hockey-stick work is a power-curve position classification of large companies. It is weak for SME targeting but strong as a message: "middle-quintile firms rarely move up without big moves."
- None of the three McKinsey works says any class of firm does not need strategic planning; Gluck et al. implicitly accept that many firms operate at phase 1-2.

### Gaps
- Full texts of the 1980 and 1997 HBR articles and the McKinsey retrospectives could not be fetched (mckinsey.com timed out three times). Phase value systems, level 3 label, posture and move names should be verified before quoting.
- No McKinsey publication specifically on when small or founder-led firms should start formal strategic planning was found in this session.
- Exact sample size of the power-curve study (commonly cited as about 2,400 of the world's largest companies, 2000-2014) was not verified.

## BCG: strategy styles by predictability, malleability and harshness

### Takeaway
BCG's "strategy palette" is the most explicit consulting classification of *which kind* of strategy-making a firm needs: style is chosen by how predictable the environment is, how malleable it is, and how harsh it is. It prescribes different planning cycles per style (multi-year plans for classical, short or continual cycles for adaptive and shaping), and its survey found most firms default to annual, classical-style planning even when it does not fit.

### Cited Findings
**Reeves, Love and Tillmanns (2012), "Your Strategy Needs a Strategy", HBR September 2012, republished by BCG 16 October 2012** [PRIMARY-READ]
- Note on authorship: the 2012 HBR article is by Martin Reeves, Claire Love and Philipp Tillmanns. Reeves, Haanaes and Sinha are the authors of the 2015 book — [BCG publication page](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Two dimensions: predictability of the environment and malleability (how far the firm or rivals can shape it); crossing them gives four styles; a fifth, survival, applies in harsh conditions — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Classical (predictable, hard to change): goal-driven, with "orderly, successive rounds of planning" and quantitative forecasts; plans often span several years — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Adaptive (unpredictable, little power to shape): shorter cycles, plans treated as rough hypotheses, strategy embedded in operations; cycles may become "continual" — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Shaping (unpredictable but malleable): short or continual cycles, a portfolio of experiments, ecosystem-building, with "little reliance is placed on elaborate prediction mechanisms" — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Visionary (predictable and shapeable by the firm): deliberate, thorough planning around a clear goal with committed resources — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Survival (severely restricted capital or resources, e.g., sharp downturn): short-term and defensive — cutting costs, preserving capital, trimming portfolios — and a growth plan to prepare for after the crisis — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Survey: about 120 companies in 10 sectors. Firms using the style that matched their environment had three-year TSR 4-8% higher on average — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- About three in four executives recognised the need for different styles, but about 75% used only classical or visionary styles in practice (reported usage: visionary 40%, classical 35%, adaptive 16%, shaping 9%) — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- Nearly 90% planned annually regardless of the real pace of change; about 70% valued accuracy over speed; fewer than one in five felt competent in adaptive capabilities — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)
- The article does not say any style needs no planning; it notes Zara does not rely heavily on a formal planning process, as an adaptive example — [BCG, 2012](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy)

**Reeves, Haanaes and Sinha (2015), "Your Strategy Needs a Strategy: How to Choose and Execute the Right Approach" (Harvard Business Review Press)**
- The book extends the palette to six approaches: classical, adaptive, visionary, shaping, renewal (replacing "survival") and ambidexterity (combining styles across units or over time); harshness is added as a third dimension — researcher's knowledge of the book, not verified in this session [PRIMARY-UNREAD; no URL fetched]

### Inferences
- This is the most directly usable model for "which kind of planning" segmentation. A vendor could score prospects on three observable dimensions: sector volatility (predictability), market concentration and firm's market power (malleability), and financial stress (harshness). Each segment maps to a product mode: annual multi-year plan (classical/visionary), rolling hypothesis-driven planning (adaptive), experiment portfolio and ecosystem tracking (shaping), and turnaround / cash planning (renewal).
- The survey finding that nearly 90% plan annually regardless of environment is a ready-made sales argument for adaptive or continuous planning features.
- The model implies some firms (adaptive, shaping) need *less elaborate prediction-based* planning, but not *no* planning.
- The survey sample is large global firms; applicability to Turkish SMEs is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- BCG Henderson Institute follow-ups (e.g., later work on adaptive advantage or strategy in uncertain times) were not retrieved in this session.
- The six-style book version and the harshness dimension were not verified from a primary page in this session.
- BCG growth-share matrix was not researched: it classifies business units for portfolio allocation, not firms by planning need, so it is judged out of scope.

## Bain: continuous issue-based planning, Management Tools survey, and Founder's Mentality stages

### Takeaway
Bain contributes (a) the Mankins-Steele argument that annual, unit-by-unit planning should give way to continuous, issue-focused decision-making (based only on $1B+ firms), (b) survey data showing strategic planning is the most-used management tool but with falling usage, and (c) the Founder's Mentality growth crises (overload, stall-out, free fall), which work as a life-stage classification of when strategy work becomes urgent.

### Cited Findings
**Mankins and Steele (2006), "Stop Making Plans; Start Making Decisions", HBR January 2006** (research by Marakon Associates with the Economist Intelligence Unit; Mankins later joined Bain, which republishes the article)
- Survey of 156 senior executives at companies with sales of $1 billion or more; four in ten had revenues over $10 billion — [CFO.com](https://www.cfo.com/news/planning-and-decision-making-out-of-sync/677469/) [SECONDARY]; [Management-Issues](https://management-issues.com/news/2893/strategic-planning-obstructs-decision-making/) [SECONDARY]
- 66% ran strategic planning periodically, usually annually, but 100% said they make strategic decisions continuously; 70% said they decide issue by issue — same secondary sources [SECONDARY]
- Executives often sidestep the formal planning process and make ad hoc decisions on thin analysis — [Management-Issues](https://management-issues.com/news/2893/strategic-planning-obstructs-decision-making/) [SECONDARY]
- Prescribed model: link decision-making and planning (identify value-creating decisions first, then use traditional planning for the implementation roadmap); focus on companywide issues; develop strategy continuously through the year instead of in a 2-3 month window ("Debate one issue at a time until you've reached a decision"); structure reviews from facts to alternatives to choices — [Bain republication of the HBR article](https://www.bain.com/insights/stop-making-plans-start-making-decisions/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- Examples: Boeing, Microsoft, Textron (reviewing two to three units per quarter) — [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/stop-making-plans-start-making-decisions/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- The Bain page does not say which types of company need which kind of planning — [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/stop-making-plans-start-making-decisions/) [PRIMARY-READ]

**Bain Management Tools & Trends survey (Rigby and Bilodeau)**
- 2017 edition (published 5 April 2018, 1,268 managers): strategic planning ranked No. 1 by global usage, used by 48% of respondents, versus 88% of managers in 2006 — [Bain, Management Tools & Trends 2017](https://www.bain.com/insights/management-tools-and-trends-2017/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- The 2017 text says satisfaction "can vary widely depending on company size and region" but gives no strategic-planning numbers by size or region in the text (only in figures not readable) — [Bain 2017](https://www.bain.com/insights/management-tools-and-trends-2017/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- In the 2011 edition, benchmarking displaced strategic planning as the most-used tool for the first time in a decade — [Bain, Management Tools & Trends 2011](https://www.bain.com/insights/management-tools-trends-2011) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Latin American companies used strategic planning and growth-strategy tools significantly more than other regions; the 2009 edition found European executives used strategic planning less than peers elsewhere — [Bain, 2009](https://www.bain.com/it/insights/management-tools-and-trends-2009/) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]; [Consultancy.uk](https://www.consultancy.uk/news/60/bain-managers-use-less-management-tools-and-models) [SECONDARY]
- Cumulative database: 7,283 respondents from over 70 countries (as of 2017 edition) — [Bain 2017](https://www.bain.com/insights/management-tools-and-trends-2017/) [snippet]

**Zook and Allen (2016), "The Founder's Mentality" (HBR Press) and Bain brief "Barriers and Pathways to Sustainable Growth" (19 July 2016)** [PRIMARY-READ]
- Founder's Mentality has three traits: insurgent mission, obsession with the front line, owner's mindset — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)
- "Scale insurgents" (target state) are about 7% of companies but over 50% of net stock-market value created — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)
- Overload (crisis of youth): bottlenecks and breakdowns during rapid scale-up, typically at 5-10x growth; top causes are revenue outpacing talent (55%) and an unscalable founder (37%); about one in 17,000 companies grows to $500M and becomes a sustained value creator — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)
- Stall-out (crisis of incumbency): mature growth engine loses momentum; causes include loss of mission (43%), unchecked complexity (42%), bureaucratisation (41%); about two-thirds of $5B+ companies expected to stall, fail, be acquired or break apart within 15 years; only one in seven recovers; about 80% of recoverers returned to the core, about 20% redefined the model — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)
- Free fall (crisis of late maturity): business model no longer works; about 5-7% of companies at any time; only 10-15% recover — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)
- The Bain page gives no stage-specific planning playbook for overload or free fall — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/)

### Inferences
- Founder's Mentality is the most SME- and founder-relevant consulting classification found. The overload stage (5-10x growth, revenue outpacing talent, unscalable founder) is a natural trigger for adopting formal planning; stall-out triggers core-refocus strategy; free fall triggers renewal. A vendor could target fast-growing founder-led firms entering overload.
- Mankins-Steele implies that large firms need continuous, issue-based strategy, not *more* annual planning; it was based only on $1B+ companies, so it says nothing about SMEs.
- Bain survey: falling strategic-planning usage (88% in 2006 to 48% in 2017) signals that the classic annual tool is losing ground, not that the need is gone.

### Gaps
- No Bain data on strategic-planning usage by company size was found in readable text.
- No newer Management Tools & Trends edition (after 2017) with strategic-planning figures was found.
- The original HBR text of Mankins-Steele was not read; survey percentages come from secondary press.

## Big Four and Strategy&: capabilities-driven strategy, Playing to Win, maturity models

### Takeaway
Strategy& (PwC) offers the "coherence" lens (fit between a firm's way to play, capabilities system and product portfolio), which classifies firms by coherence rather than by planning need. Monitor Deloitte uses Lafley and Martin's "Playing to Win" choice cascade, a method not a classification. No published strategic-planning maturity model from Deloitte, EY or KPMG was found; the widely cited five-level model comes from the Balanced Scorecard Institute, not a Big Four firm.

### Cited Findings
- Leinwand and Mainardi (2010/2011), "The Essential Advantage: How to Win with a Capabilities-Driven Strategy" (HBR Press; Booz & Company, now Strategy&): success goes to firms with a "coherence premium", a tight fit between strategic direction and distinctive capabilities — [Strategy& book page](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/gx/en/insights/books/the-essential-advantage.html) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]; [Leinwand and Mainardi, "The Coherence Premium", HBR 2010, PDF on Strategy&](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/gx/en/insights/2002-2013/coherence-premium/hbr-coherence-premium.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- Coherence is measured by counting capabilities common across the segments a firm serves and comparing that with EBIT margin; authors suggest focusing on at most about six capabilities — [StrategyDriven interview with the authors](https://strategydriven.com/?p=9835) [SECONDARY]; [Consulting Magazine review](https://www.consultingmag.com/amp/2011/03/25/review-the-essential-advantage/) [SECONDARY]
- A blog review criticised the book as itself less coherent than its message — [Lifetime Reading blog, 2012](https://lifetimereading.blogspot.com/2012/07/the-essential-advantage-how-to-win-with.html) [SECONDARY]
- Lafley and Martin (2013), "Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works" (HBR Press): five cascading choices (winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, required capabilities, management systems); Monitor Group (now Monitor Deloitte) is associated with the method — researcher's knowledge; no page fetched in this session [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- A search for "strategic planning maturity" models from KPMG, EY or Deloitte returned none; the frequently cited five-level Strategic Management Maturity Model (ad hoc and static, reactive, structured and proactive, managed and focused, continuous improvement) is from the Balanced Scorecard Institute (2010) — [Balanced Scorecard Institute, "How to Assess Your Strategic Management Maturity", 2022](https://balancedscorecard.org/staging/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/How-to-Assess-Your-Strategic-Management-Maturity-August-2022.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]; [TIM Review 2018 paper citing it](https://www.timreview.ca/node/1196) [SECONDARY]
- One aggregator claims "Deloitte research" shows mature planning frameworks implement strategy 51% faster; no Deloitte source could be found, so the claim is unverified — [search result, rework.com](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/organizational-competencies/strategic-planning) [SECONDARY; unreliable]

### Inferences
- Coherence (Strategy&) suggests a targeting angle: diversified firms with many segments and scattered capabilities ("incoherent") need strategy work most. This is an inference, not a Strategy& segmentation.
- Playing to Win is a template a SaaS could embed as a workflow, not a customer classification.
- For a "planning maturity" diagnostic, the vendor would need to cite the Balanced Scorecard Institute model or Gluck et al. (1980), not a Big Four model.

### Gaps
- EY and KPMG strategy or planning maturity models: none found. This is an absence of evidence from one search, not proof that none exists; direct site searches were not done due to the tool-call budget.
- Strategy& "Fit for Growth" and later Leinwand works (e.g., "Strategy That Works", 2016; "Beyond Digital", 2022) were not reviewed.
- Deloitte Insights material on strategy for mid-market or private companies was not reviewed.

## Consulting-firm guidance for SMEs, family businesses and founder-led firms

### Takeaway
The only quantified consulting-firm evidence found is PwC's Family Business Survey: a large share of family businesses lack a formal strategic plan (64% in Ireland vs 51% globally, 2019), and PwC links good plans to higher growth. Apart from Bain's Founder's Mentality stages, no major firm was found to publish a rule for when an SME should start formal strategic planning.

### Cited Findings
- PwC 2016 Global Family Business Survey described a "missing middle": many family firms lack a plan linking current position to long-term goals — [PwC Global Family Business Survey 2016 (Luxembourg PDF)](https://www.pwc.lu/en/pwc-entrepreneur/docs/pwc-global-family-business-survey-2016.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]; [PwC Singapore release, "Lack of strategic planning will hold family businesses back"](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/sg/pwc-singapore/documents/lack-of-strategic-planning-will-hold-family-businesses-back-from-growth-pwc-295572) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- 2019: 64% of Irish family businesses had no formalised strategic plan, versus 51% of global peers; firms with good strategic plans showed higher growth — [PwC 2019 Irish Family Business Report](https://aib.ie/content/dam/frontdoor/business/docs/my-business-is/family-business/pwc-2019-irish-family-business-report.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Succession planning gaps (proxy for formal planning): only 23% of US family businesses described their succession plan as robust, documented and communicated (2016, down from 27% in 2014); 91% of Middle East family businesses lacked a formally documented succession plan vs 86% globally — [Family Business Magazine on PwC US 2016](https://www.familybusinessmagazine.com/sites/default/files/articles/2017/03/20/06OpenersMarch-April2017.html) [SECONDARY]; [Saudi Gazette on PwC Middle East](https://saudigazette.com.sa/article/168672) [SECONDARY]
- PwC 2025 US Family Business Survey: 70% have documented family visions (not the same as a strategic plan) — [PwC 2025 US Family Business Survey PDF](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/services/audit-assurance/private-company-services/library/assets/pwc-2025-family-business-survey.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD, snippet]
- Bain's Founder's Mentality (2016) is the only major-firm stage model aimed at founder-led growth; see the Bain section — [Bain, 2016](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/) [PRIMARY-READ]

### Inferences
- PwC data support a targeting claim: about half of family businesses globally (2019) had no formal strategic plan, so family firms are an under-served segment. Turkey-specific PwC family business data were not checked.
- Combining PwC (formal-plan gap) and Bain (overload trigger) gives a sourced rationale for targeting growing, founder- or family-led firms, though neither firm frames it as a planning-need classification.

### Gaps
- No McKinsey, BCG, Deloitte, EY or KPMG guidance on the threshold (size, age, growth) at which an SME should adopt formal strategic planning was found.
- PwC Turkey family business survey figures were not retrieved.
- The full 2025 PwC Global Family Business Survey section on strategic planning was not read.

## Summary matrix: dimensions, classes, need implied, and targeting use

### Takeaway
Across firms, three classification axes recur: planning-system maturity (McKinsey 1980), environmental uncertainty or malleability (McKinsey 1997, BCG 2012/2015), and growth or life stage (Bain 2016). No firm classifies by legal SME size; none states a class of firm needs no strategic planning.

### Cited Findings
- Gluck, Kaufman, Walleck 1980 (McKinsey/HBR): dimension = maturity of planning system; 4 phases; implies firms climb stage by stage — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/thinking-strategically) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- Courtney, Kirkland, Viguerie 1997 (McKinsey/HBR): dimension = residual uncertainty; 4 levels; level 1 tolerates single-forecast planning, higher levels need scenarios and postures — [McKinsey PDF](https://mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Featured%20Insights/Managing%20In%20Uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty/Strategy%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- Reeves, Love, Tillmanns 2012 (BCG/HBR): dimensions = predictability, malleability (+ harshness); 5 styles; different planning cycles per style — [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2012/your-strategy-needs-a-strategy) [PRIMARY-READ]
- Mankins, Steele 2006 (Marakon, republished by Bain/HBR): not a classification; prescribes continuous, issue-based planning for large firms — [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/stop-making-plans-start-making-decisions/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- Zook, Allen 2016 (Bain): dimension = growth stage; insurgency, overload, stall-out, free fall — [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/founders-mentality-barriers-and-pathways-to-sustainable-growth/) [PRIMARY-READ]
- Bradley, Hirt, Smit 2018 (McKinsey): dimension = economic-profit position; quintiles on a power curve — [McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategy-to-beat-the-odds) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]
- Leinwand, Mainardi 2010 (Strategy&): dimension = coherence of capabilities across segments — [Strategy&](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/gx/en/insights/books/the-essential-advantage.html) [PRIMARY-UNREAD]

### Inferences
- A defensible, fully attributed targeting scheme could combine three cited axes: Bain growth stage (when), BCG environment style (which kind), and Gluck phase (how mature the current process is). The combination itself would be the vendor's construction, and should be labelled as such.
- Most of these models were built on large firms (McKinsey 1980, Mankins-Steele $1B+, BCG ~120 large firms, Hockey Stick large caps). Applying them to Turkish SMEs is extrapolation.
